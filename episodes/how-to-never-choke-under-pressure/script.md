# How to never choke under pressure
Promise: play closer to your practice level when it matters most
Framework: what choking is, then four skills: train the pressure, reframe the nerves, lock in, reset

## Hook
Match point. You've hit this serve a thousand times in practice. Now your arm feels like it belongs to someone else.

Or it's a penalty to win the game, and your legs forget how to kick.

That's choking. It can happen to anyone, from club players to superstars. [S10][S16]

Nerves won't disappear. But you can learn to play your best with them. Today I'll show you four skills that help you do exactly that.

## Why you choke
Choking means playing worse than you normally would, right when it matters most. [S10][S19]

Scientists have two main explanations. [S10][S19]

The first is overthinking. Under pressure, you start controlling a move that normally runs on autopilot, and it falls apart. [S10][S14]

It's like singing a song you know by heart, but trying to remember every single note. You stumble.

The second is worry. Worry eats up your attention and drags it toward threats, like the goalkeeper, the crowd or the score. [S15][S12]

In one study, anxious penalty takers looked at the goalkeeper sooner and for longer, and shot closer to him. [S12][S21]

And mistakes snowball. In over 650,000 Grand Slam tennis points, pressure and a previous error both made the next error more likely. [S16]

So here's the plan. Train the pressure. Reframe the nerves. Lock in. And reset after mistakes. [S1][S19]

## Train the pressure
Skill one: practise with something at stake, so the big day feels familiar. [S2]

Think of it like a vaccine. A small, safe dose of pressure in training, so the real thing doesn't knock you over.

A review of 14 studies found pressure training beat normal training when it came to performing under pressure. [S2]

It worked for beginners and experienced players, and for different kinds of skills. [S2]

In a classic experiment, novice golfers who practised putting while being filmed and judged didn't choke later. The golfers who practised normally did. [S14]

Here's how to build it. Drill one: consequence sets. Take 10 penalties, free throws or short serves. Every miss costs a small forfeit, or goes on a leaderboard. [S2]

Drill two: film yourself, and have a teammate score you out of 10. [S14]

Drill three: must-make ladders. Make 5 in a row, or start again. Or play first-to-3 shootouts against a teammate. [S2][S12]

A good starting point is one or two sets, once or twice a week, at the end of a normal session.

Keep the forfeits light and friendly. Never humiliating, and never extra exercise when you're already exhausted.

The common mistake? Only practising in calm, easy drills, and hoping more reps will be enough. In the studies, the same practice done under pressure beat calm practice. [S2]

## Reframe the nerves
Skill two: your heart is pounding before the big moment. That's normal. What matters is how you read it. [S9]

It's the same engine with a different dashboard light. The revs are high either way. You decide whether it says danger or ready.

Scientists call this threat versus challenge. Both come with high arousal. In a challenge state, you believe you have what it takes. [S9]

In a study of 60 experienced golfers, the ones guided into a challenge mindset putted better, and did less overthinking. [S9]

Most people try to calm down. In one survey, over 90% thought that was the best tactic. [S8]

But in one experiment, people who said "I'm excited" out loud before singing karaoke sang more accurately than people who said "I'm anxious". In a separate check, saying it didn't slow their racing hearts at all. [S8]

To be fair, those studies were about singing, public speaking and maths, not sport. And in sport, the few studies of quick reframing cues haven't shown a clear benefit yet. So treat it as a free tool worth trying, not a proven fix. [S8][S1]

Drill one: before your key moment, say it out loud. I'm excited. This is my body getting ready. [S8]

Drill two: before a match, write down 3 things you've done in training that prepare you for it. [S9]

What about deep breathing? Slow breathing does shift your body toward rest. But for sport performance, the results are mixed. [S17][S18]

So use one slow breath as part of your routine, not as a cure. [S12]

And if your nerves are constant, cause panic attacks, or stop you sleeping, eating or wanting to play, talk to a sport psychologist or a doctor. If you're under 18, tell a coach or parent too. [S20]

## Lock in
Skill three: one routine, one look, one cue. [S3][S4]

A routine is the same short set of actions before every key shot, like a pilot's checklist that ends with eyes on the runway. [S3]

A big review found routines improved performance, and the benefit held up under pressure. [S3]

Then the look. The quiet eye is your final, steady look at the target just before you move. [S4]

Experts hold it longer than beginners. And across training studies, practising the quiet eye improved performance. [S4]

In one football study, players who called out their corner, held their eyes on it for about 1 second, and then kicked were the only group to improve in a pressure shootout. [S13]

And don't rush. In major tournament shootouts, players who started their run-up within about half a second of the whistle scored 68% of the time. [S10]

Players who waited a moment longer scored 82%. Rushing might be a sign of nerves, but either way, slow down. [S10]

Finally, one cue word, about the target, not your body. Something like corner, through or smooth. Self-talk like this helps, especially for precise skills. [S7][S14]

Drill one: build a 4-step routine. Set up, breathe, eyes on target, cue word. Then go. Use it on every rep in training, not just in matches. [S3][S12]

Drill two: the quiet-eye drill. Call your target, hold your eyes on it for about 1 second, then go. Start with at least 10 reps a session, and keep it up for at least 3 weeks. That's roughly what one small football study used. [S13][S4]

The common mistake is changing your routine in the big moment, or speeding it up to get it over with. [S10][S12]

## Reset after mistakes
Skill four: one mistake raises the chance of another, especially on the big points. That's the spiral. [S16]

A reset is like closing all your browser tabs. Close the last point, and open one fresh tab for the next.

This part has less direct research behind it. It takes what works for routines and self-talk, and uses it between points. [S3][S7]

The reset has three steps. Release: turn away, or wipe your hands. Breathe: one slow breath out. Refocus: one cue word for the next play. [S3][S7][S12]

Then go straight into your normal routine.

Drill one: in training games, run the reset after every single error, so it becomes automatic.

Drill two: write a what-if plan. If I miss, I'll jog back, clap my teammate, and say next. [S12]

Drill three: start practice games from a bad score, like 0-30 or two goals down. [S2][S16]

Keep your self-talk kind and useful. Beating yourself up is not a reset. [S7]

The common mistake is replaying the error, or chasing it with a risky shot. Errors cluster under pressure, so break the chain. [S16]

## Your week
Here's a simple week.

Early in the week, write your routine, your cue word and your what-if plan. Practise the routine with quiet eye, 3 sets of 10. [S3][S13]

Midweek, a pressure block. Ten reps with consequences, filmed, with the routine on every rep and a reset after every miss. [S2][S14]

Later, play games from bad scores, and finish with a must-make ladder. [S2][S16]

On match day, use the same routine on every key moment. Don't rush, and reset after every mistake. [S10][S12]

## Close
Before the match, train the pressure and reframe the nerves. During it, lock in. After a mistake, reset.

The nerves will still show up at match point. This time, you'll know exactly what to do with them.
