# Research brief — How to never get tired in badminton

Audience: amateur and club players, 16–40. Every factual line carries [S#] → `sources.json`.
Lines marked **(coach-derived)** are practical prescriptions I built from match data plus general guidelines. They are not tested badminton protocols. The script should present them as "a good starting point", not "studies show".

---

## Promise
Finish the third game as quick to the shuttle as in the first, by training the two energy systems badminton actually runs on (plus the legs that carry them).

### Does the "two energy systems" framing hold up? Mostly, yes, with one honest footnote
- Lab data from match play led researchers to conclude that badminton depends on **both anaerobic alactic (phosphocreatine, "PCr") and aerobic energy** [S2]. Reviews say the same: roughly **60–70% aerobic and ~30% anaerobic**, with the anaerobic part coming **mainly from the alactic system rather than the lactic system** [S1]. Coaching recommendations from Olympic-final data also call for training "both aerobic capacity and the anaerobic alactic system" [S4].
- **Footnote:** the lactic (glycolytic) system isn't zero. Average match lactate is modest (~1.9 mmol/L in one lab match [S2]; a mean of ~4.4 across studies, range 2.9–12.2 [S1]), so it plays a part in long, brutal rallies. The script can say "two main engines" and add one line: "your third engine only kicks in during the really long rallies."
- The 60–70 / 30 split is a review-level estimate drawn from older studies [S1]. Say "about two-thirds", not a precise figure.

## Framework (suggested: 3 engines/parts + 1 habit chapter)

| # | Part | What it is | Why it's in the video |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The Sprint Battery** (alactic / PCr system) | Instant energy for each 5–10 s rally: the lunge, the jump, the push back to base | Rallies are short and explosive [S1][S2][S3] |
| 2 | **The Recharger** (aerobic system) | Refills the sprint battery in the 10–25 s between rallies, and supplies most of the total energy | Aerobic fitness speeds PCr recovery and power recovery across repeated efforts [S9]. Most of the energy is aerobic [S1] |
| 3 | **The Shock Absorbers** (leg strength and tendon resilience) | Strong legs make each lunge and landing cost less and protect the knees and Achilles | Lunges and jumps happen ~100–200 times a match [S3][S6]. Strength training cuts sports injuries [S15]. Achilles ruptures happen late in games [S18] |
| 4 | **Between-point habits** (match-day recovery) | Using the rest you're allowed, drinking, fuelling long sessions | Rest periods are where recovery happens [S1][S21]. Dehydration >2–3% hurts performance [S20] |

Parts 1–2 are the "two energy systems" in the title. Part 3 is not an energy system. It's what experts list alongside them as determinants of playing ability (strength, explosive power, muscular endurance, agility) [S1][S13], and it's the main safety lever. Part 4 can be a short chapter or part of "Put it together".

Hero `custom` scene idea: a two-tank diagram. A small "sprint battery" drains in a 7 s rally, then a big "aerobic recharger" pipe refills it during the 15 s rest. In game 3 the refill is slower in an unfit player and full in a fit one [S1][S9].

## Part 1 — The Sprint Battery (alactic / PCr)

**Explanation.** Every rally is a short burst. Averages run from about 5.5 s in a lab match [S2], to ~7 s as a typical figure [S1], to ~9 s in pooled elite singles [S3]. Short, all-out efforts like these are powered mainly by anaerobic energy, which dominates any maximal effort shorter than about 75–80 s [S10]. In badminton the anaerobic share comes mainly from the fast, no-lactate phosphocreatine system [S1].

**Analogy.** A camera flash: a huge burst of power instantly, but it needs a few seconds to recharge before the next flash. (The recharge is Part 2.)

**Sport moments (mascot-actable).**
- Split-step, then explosive lunge to a net drop (`lunge` pose) [S6]
- Jump smash from the rear court (`jump`) [S3][S7]
- Scrambling back to base after a deep lift (`sprint` cycle)
- A single men's singles match had ~46 full lunges, ~52 half-lunges and ~62 split-steps [S6]

**Training methods + dosage (coach-derived, built from match work:rest).** The match structure is roughly 1:2 to 1:3 work:rest. Typical figures are 7 s on / 15 s off [S1], 9 s / 17–23 s in pooled elite singles [S3], and ~1:3 recommended for elite metabolic training [S4]. Repeated sprints and all-out efforts are an effective high-intensity format for racket-sport players [S11].
1. **Shadow-footwork "rally repeats".** Move to a called or random corner and back to base, at full speed with clean technique, for **8–10 s**, then rest **20–30 s** (≈1:3). Do **8–10 reps × 2–3 sets**, with **3 min easy between sets**, **1–2×/week** **(coach-derived; ratio from [S1][S4])**. Beginners: 6 reps × 2 sets at 1:4.
2. **Multi-shuttle feeding (with a partner).** The feeder sends ~8–12 shuttles fast to all corners (~10 s), the player rests ~30 s, and they swap roles. Do **2 × 8 reps**, 1×/week **(coach-derived)**. Small trials of multi-shuttle HIIT report better agility and VO2max, but the evidence is weak [S12].
3. **Reaction starts.** Split-step on a partner's point, then a 2–3-step explosive push to the indicated corner. Do **5–6 reps × 3 sets**, with **~20–30 s** between reps and **2 min** between sets. Keep it fast and fresh, and stop when speed drops **(coach-derived)**.

**Not suitable / caution:** anyone returning from a calf, Achilles, knee or ankle injury (clear it with a physio first). Complete beginners should build 3–4 weeks of Part 2 and Part 3 work first. Don't do these on consecutive days with heavy plyometrics [S16].

**Common mistake.** Doing "sprint" drills with short rests, so every rep gets slower and turns into a lactic burn. That trains the wrong engine for the purpose. Sprint quality needs real rest; match-like rest is ~2–3× the work [S1][S3][S4].

## Part 2 — The Recharger (aerobic system)

**Explanation.** Between rallies the aerobic system works hard to rebuild your sprint fuel. Fitter players regenerate phosphocreatine faster and keep their power better across repeated sprints [S9]. Researchers who measured real matches concluded that a well-developed aerobic endurance is needed "for fast recovery between rallies" and to tolerate intensive training [S2]. This engine also supplies most of a match's energy (~60–70%) [S1]. Heart rate stays very high the whole time: ~89% of peak in a lab match [S2], and >90% of max across studies [S1]. Even recreational singles is vigorous: ~76% of VO2max and a mean HR of ~168 bpm in 30-min matches by ~36-year-old club men [S8].

**Analogy.** The phone charger. The battery (Part 1) gives the burst, and the charger decides how full it is when the next rally starts. A weak charger means you start each rally at 70%, then 50%…

**Sport moments (mascot-actable).**
- Walking back to serve with slow breaths while a gauge refills (`gauges`/battery icon)
- Game 3: an unfit Ember gets `hands_knees` after a long rally, while a fit Ember is already at base and ready
- In recreational players, heart rate rose in the final 10 minutes and effort ratings climbed from ~16 to ~17.6 (out of 20) between minute 15 and minute 30 [S8]. That's the "getting tired" the title promises to fix.

**Training methods + dosage.**
1. **Easy aerobic base.** Jog, cycle or swim at a "can still talk" effort (RPE ~12–13 out of 20), **30–45 min, 1–2×/week**. That sits within ACSM's moderate-intensity range of 30–60 min [S14]. Progress duration before intensity [S14].
2. **Long intervals (aerobic power).** **4 × 3–4 min hard** (RPE ~15–17, "hard but controlled"), with **2–3 min easy** between, **1×/week**. The ACSM vigorous range is RPE 14–17 [S14]. Buchheit & Laursen's aim is several minutes per session near VO2max [S11]. The exact 4×3–4 min set is **coach-derived**.
3. **Badminton-specific intermittent blocks.** Shadow footwork at a strong but sustainable pace, **15 s on / 15 s off for 8–10 min**, × **2 blocks** with 3–4 min rest, **1×/week** **(coach-derived; short-interval HIT principle [S11])**. Unlike Part 1 this is not all-out. The aim is to keep heart rate high for minutes.
- Evidence check: in badminton players, HIIT and sprint-interval training improved VO2max in a 2025 meta-analysis (SMD 1.27). That result rests on only **2 small studies** (32 players), over 4–10 weeks at 2–3 sessions/week [S12]. Present it as "early evidence".
- Build it up gradually: progress intensity, duration and frequency step by step [S14].

**Not suitable / caution:** hard intervals (methods 2–3) are for healthy players. Anyone with chest pain, fainting, unusual breathlessness or a known heart condition should see a doctor before vigorous training (general caution; see Safety).

**Common mistake.** "I'll just play more games." Match play is vigorous [S8], but it's unstructured. Pure long slow jogging misses the repeated-sprint demand [S1][S2]. Pure HIIT with no base leaves the recharger weak [S2][S9]. You need both.

## Part 3 — The Shock Absorbers (strength and tendon resilience)

**Explanation.** Lunging makes up ~18% of all singles movements [S1]. One study counted 160–240 lunges per elite men's singles match [S3], and another counted ~46 full and ~52 half lunges per match at a continental championship [S6]. Singles has ~40% more jumps and ~250% more lunges than doubles [S7]. Resistance training in badminton players improves power, speed, agility and endurance [S13]. In general sport, strength training cut injuries to **less than one-third** (RR 0.315) [S15]. Achilles ruptures in badminton mostly hit **recreational** players (97% recreational or beginners, mean age 36), and **94% happened in the middle or at the end of a game**. The authors suggest muscle fatigue may play a part [S18].

**Analogy.** Car suspension. Stronger shocks soak up every pothole (lunge or landing) with less wear, so the engine wastes less fuel and the chassis doesn't crack late in the race.

**Sport moments (mascot-actable).**
- Deep net lunge and push back out (`lunge`, callout on quads and glutes)
- Landing after a jump smash (callout on the calf and Achilles)
- Scissor-kick landing with the foot turned out (see the safety tip)

**Training methods + dosage** (ACSM resistance ranges for novice to intermediate adults: 2–3 days/week, 2–4 sets × 8–12 reps at a moderate-hard load, 2–3 min rest, 48 h between sessions for the same muscles [S14]).
1. **Split squat → reverse lunge → (later) loaded forward lunge**: **2–3 × 8–12 per leg**, **2×/week** [S14]. Typical badminton resistance programmes ran 2–3×/week for 4–14 weeks [S13].
2. **Calf raises**, straight-knee and bent-knee, slow and controlled: **3 × 12–15**, **2–3×/week**. Progress from two legs to one leg to added weight. Calf and Achilles strength is the target because of the Achilles risk [S18]. The exact dosage follows the ACSM ranges [S14] **(coach-derived)**.
3. **Low-level plyometrics** (pogo hops, lateral line hops, small split jumps): **60–100 foot contacts per session**, **2×/week**, **48+ h apart**. 80–100 contacts is the usual novice recommendation, and lower volumes worked as well as double the volume [S16].
- **Landing tip:** after forehand jump strokes, rotate the upper body so the non-racket foot lands turned out (as elite players do in the scissor kick). This reduced estimated Achilles load by ~25% in a small lab study [S19].

**Not suitable / caution:** anyone with current knee, Achilles or calf pain should get it assessed before plyometrics. Under-16s should be supervised by a qualified coach and do bodyweight work first.

**Common mistake.** Stretching instead of strengthening. Stretching programmes showed no injury reduction (RR 0.96), while strength training showed the largest effect [S15]. ACSM likewise notes that the link between flexibility and fewer injuries hasn't been substantiated [S14].

## Part 4 — Between-point and match-day habits

**Explanation.** Recovery happens in the gaps: ~10–25 s between rallies [S2][S3], intervals of up to 60 s at 11 points, and up to 120 s between games (current 3×21 Laws) [S21]. Elite rest times have grown much longer over the years (14.8 s → 33.5 s in Olympic finals), which the authors read as players needing more recovery as the game intensifies [S4]. Players lose sweat at about 1.1 L/h [S1]. Dehydration beyond 2–3% of body mass decreases performance [S20].

**Analogy.** Pit stops. A Formula 1 car isn't faster for skipping them.

**Habits (mascot-actable: walking back slowly, breathing, drinking, banana in the bag).**
1. **Use your rest, legally.** Walk back, reset and breathe before the serve. Don't rush to serve when you're gassed. Rule caps: 60 s at 11 and 120 s between games [S21]. Slow-breathing benefits specifically: see Unverified.
2. **Drink to match sweat, not more.** Drink at intervals and between games. Players have plenty of chances to rehydrate [S1]. Avoid both >2–3% body-mass loss and drinking beyond sweat rate [S20].
3. **Fuel long sessions.** For play lasting over an hour (tournaments, long club nights), take **~30–60 g carbohydrate per hour** (sports drink, banana, gel) [S20]. For a single 40-minute match it's usually unnecessary (the guideline is aimed at >1 h) [S20].

**Common mistake.** Treating the 120 s between games as dead time. That's your biggest refill window, so drink, breathe and reset tactics [S21][S1].

## Put it together — sample week (coach-derived, for a player with ~3 sessions/week of available time beyond club play)

| Day | Session |
|---|---|
| Mon | Club play + 10 min Part 1 rally repeats beforehand (warm-up first) |
| Tue | Strength: split squats, calf raises, 60–80 plyo contacts (Part 3) |
| Wed | Easy aerobic base 30–40 min (Part 2) |
| Thu | Club play |
| Fri | Strength (lighter) + 15:15 shadow intermittent block (Part 2) |
| Sat | Long intervals 4×3–4 min, or match play |
| Sun | Rest |

Rules: strength 2×/week, 48 h apart [S14]; plyos ≤2×/week [S16]; build for 4–10 weeks, which is the window in which badminton training studies show changes [S12]. Hard days and easy days alternate. Never do Part 1 sprints when you're already exhausted.

## Myths

1. **"Badminton fitness = lots of long runs."** The match is repeated short sprints with short rests, so you need alactic power as well as aerobic capacity [S1][S2][S4]. Long runs help the recharger but not the sprint battery.
2. **"Just do HIIT, cardio is outdated."** The aerobic system supplies most of the energy [S1] and drives recovery between rallies [S2][S9]. HIIT is one tool, and the badminton-specific evidence for it is still thin [S12].
3. **"The burn / lactic acid is what tires you in badminton."** Average match lactate is fairly low (~1.9 mmol/L in a lab match [S2]; ~4.4 mean across studies [S1]). The main demand is alactic plus aerobic [S1]. Lactate does rise in long rallies, so don't say it's "zero".
4. **"Stretching prevents injuries."** It doesn't in RCT pooling (RR 0.96). Strength training does (RR 0.32) [S15][S14].
5. **"Only elite players get hurt."** Achilles ruptures cluster in recreational players in their 30s, late in games [S18][S19].
6. **"Doubles and singles need the same conditioning."** Singles has higher heart rates, more steps, more lunges and more jumps [S5][S7]. Men's doubles rallies are shorter with longer rests [S3][S7].

## Safety (for the script and the fact-checker)

- **General:** educational content for healthy amateurs, not medical advice. Anyone with chest pain, dizziness or fainting during exercise, a known heart condition, or current joint or tendon pain should check with a doctor or physio before hard intervals or plyometrics. (General caution. ACSM's guidance is framed for "apparently healthy adults" [S14].)
- **Progression:** build gradually: intensity, duration and frequency [S14]. Start strength at the novice end (fewer sets, moderate loads) [S14]. Start plyos at ≤80–100 contacts, 2×/week, 48 h+ apart [S16]. Allow 4–10 weeks for adaptations [S12].
- **Warm-up:** do a progressive, dynamic warm-up before sprint or plyo work (e.g., light cardio, walking lunges, skips, a few submaximal sprints and jumps, as in [S16]'s protocol). Specific warm-up injury-reduction data for badminton were not found (see Unverified).
- **Achilles / calf:** the highest-consequence injury for the target viewer (recreational, 30s, late in a game) [S18][S19]. Use calf strengthening, landing technique [S19], and don't play through new calf or heel pain (16% of ruptured players had earlier local symptoms) [S18]. **Red flag:** a sudden "kicked in the heel" sensation or pop, or being unable to push off: stop and seek medical care. (Common clinical description, not sourced here, so keep it generic: "sudden heel pain or a pop: stop, get it checked".)
- **Knees:** lunges load the knee. Progress lunge depth and load gradually, and keep the knee tracking over the foot. Lower-body injuries are 41–92% of badminton injuries [S17].
- **Heat and hydration:** match fluid to sweat, avoid >2–3% loss, don't overdrink [S20].
- **Youth (16–17):** supervised strength training. Use bodyweight first.
- **No supplement claims.** Carbohydrate food or drinks only, per position stand [S20].

## Unverified (do NOT present as fact)

- **Interval rules under 3×15** (when the mid-game break happens and how long). BWF pages were blocked, so this isn't confirmed. Current 3×21 values (60 s at 11, 120 s between games) are sourced [S21]. Say "the short breaks the rules allow" rather than giving numbers, or note "under current rules".
- **Slow/nasal breathing between rallies speeds recovery.** No badminton evidence found. You can say "reset your breathing" as a routine cue, but don't claim a physiological recovery effect.
- **PCr recovery half-time (~30 s) / full recovery (~3–5 min).** These are widely quoted textbook figures, but I didn't open a source. If needed, cite only qualitatively ("partly refills in seconds, fully in minutes") or have the fact-checker source it.
- **Exact drill dosages** (8–10 s on / 20–30 s off, 15:15, 4×3–4 min) are coach-derived from the match ratios [S1][S3][S4] and HIT principles [S11]. No badminton RCT with these exact protocols was found. Small Indonesian and Malaysian footwork-HIIT studies exist, but they're low quality and weren't used.
- **Warm-up programmes reducing badminton injuries specifically.** Not found. Use the general strength/proprioception evidence [S15].
- **Achilles rupture warning signs** ("felt like being kicked") are a common clinical description, not sourced here.
- **The 30–60 g/h carbohydrate guidance** comes from the 2009 joint position stand [S20]. A 2016 update exists (Thomas, Erdman & Burke), but I didn't open it, so its exact figures aren't verified.
- **Amateur match data are scarce.** Nearly all time-motion data are elite [S3]. Club rallies are likely shorter, with more unforced errors (not verified). Use "even top players…" framing.
