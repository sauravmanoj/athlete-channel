# How to never get tired in badminton
Promise: be as quick to the shuttle in the third game as in the first
Framework: two energy engines (sprint battery + recharger), the shock absorbers that carry them, and the gaps where you refill

## Hook
It's the third game. The shuttle drops short at the net, and your legs just say no.

Your opponent looks fresh. You're bent over, gasping between every point.

You'll still get tired. Everyone does. But you can push it much later into the match.

Badminton doesn't tire you out the way running does. And once you see how it really works, you can train for it.

Today I'll show you the two engines badminton runs on, and exactly how to build each one.

## Two engines
A badminton rally is short. Even at the top level, a typical rally lasts about 7 seconds. Then you get about 15 seconds of rest. [S1]

Burst, rest. Burst, rest. Over and over, for the whole match. [S1]

And it's hard. Across studies, players' heart rate averages around 90% of their maximum for the whole match. [S1][S2]

Even club players in their thirties averaged around 168 beats per minute in a 30-minute match. [S8]

So your body runs two main engines. [S1][S2]

Engine one is the sprint battery. It powers every lunge, jump and push back to the middle. [S1]

Engine two is the recharger. It's your aerobic system, and it refills that battery between rallies. [S2][S9]

About two-thirds of the total energy actually comes from the recharger. [S1]

There's a third engine, the one behind the leg burn. It's always running a little, but in badminton it plays a smaller part. You feel it most in long, brutal rallies. [S1][S10]

## The sprint battery
Engine one is like a camera flash. Huge power, instantly, but only for a few seconds at full blast. [S24]

Think of a split step, a lunge to the net, and a push back to the middle. That's the sprint battery firing. [S6]

One study of 20 men's singles matches counted, on average, about 46 full lunges and 52 half lunges per match. [S6]

To train it, copy the rhythm of a real rally: short, fast, and then real rest. Repeated short sprints are a proven way to train racket players. [S1][S4][S11]

Quick check first. If you've had chest pain, fainting or unusual breathlessness when exercising, or you have a heart condition, see a doctor before hard training.

Drill one: shadow rally repeats. A good starting point, built from real match rhythm: move to a corner and back to the middle at full speed for 8 to 10 seconds, then rest 20 to 30 seconds. [S1][S4]

Try 8 reps, 2 or 3 sets, with 3 easy minutes between sets, once or twice a week.

Drill two: multi-shuttle feeding. A partner feeds 8 to 12 shuttles fast to all four corners. Then you swap, so you rest while they work.

Two sets of 8 rounds is plenty. Stop when your footwork gets sloppy.

Drill three: reaction starts. Split step on your partner's call, then explode 2 or 3 steps to the corner they point at. 5 or 6 reps, 3 sets, with full rest between reps.

Always warm up first: 5 minutes of easy movement, then walking lunges, skips, and a few build-up sprints and small jumps. [S16]

New to hard training? Spend 3 to 4 weeks on the aerobic and strength work first. Then start with 2 sets of 6 reps and longer rests.

The common mistake is cutting the rest short. Every rep gets slower, and it stops being sprint training. That's a different workout, which we'll get to. [S1][S4]

Quality first. If your speed drops, stop the set.

## The recharger
Engine two is the phone charger. The battery gives you the burst. The charger decides how full it is when the next rally starts.

Fitter players tend to recover their power faster between repeated sprints, and refilling that sprint fuel is a big part of why. [S9]

Researchers who tested international players in a simulated match agreed: you need a strong aerobic engine for fast recovery between rallies. [S2]

With a weak charger, you start each rally a little emptier than the last.

You can even see it in club players. In the last 10 minutes of a match, heart rate climbs and the effort feels harder. [S8]

Drill one: easy aerobic base. Jog, cycle or swim at a pace where you can still talk, for 30 to 45 minutes, once or twice a week. [S14]

Drill two: long intervals. A good starting point: 4 rounds of 3 to 4 minutes, hard but controlled, with 2 to 3 minutes easy in between, once a week. [S25][S14]

Drill three: court blocks, a good starting point. Shadow footwork for 15 seconds, rest 15 seconds, for 8 to 10 minutes. Strong, but not all out. [S11][S25]

Early studies show interval training can raise aerobic fitness in badminton players within about 4 to 10 weeks. [S12]

Build it up gradually. Add a little time or a little intensity, one step at a time, not both at once. [S14]

The mistake is picking only one. Only long jogs, and you never train your sprints. Only hard intervals, and you'll struggle to recover from them, because a solid aerobic base helps you handle hard training. You need both. [S1][S2]

## The shock absorbers
Your two engines need a chassis that can take a beating. That's your legs. [S1][S13]

Think of car suspension. Strong shocks handle every lunge and landing with room to spare.

Singles players lunge and jump far more than doubles players do. [S7]

Strength training makes badminton players more powerful, faster and better at changing direction. [S13]

In a big review of injury-prevention trials across many sports, strength training cut injuries to about a third. [S15]

That matters for one injury in particular: the Achilles tendon. [S18]

In one study, 97% of badminton Achilles ruptures happened to recreational players. Their average age was 36. [S18]

And 94% happened in the middle or at the end of a game. The researchers think tired muscles may play a part. [S18]

Drill one: split squats, building up to lunges. 2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12 per leg, twice a week. [S14]

Drill two: calf raises, with straight knees and bent knees. 3 sets of 12 to 15, slow and controlled. Move from two legs to one when it's easy. [S14][S18]

Drill three: small hops, like pogo hops and side-to-side line hops. Add them once the calf raises feel easy and pain-free, after a few weeks. [S16][S18]

Start at the low end, around 60 easy contacts, twice a week with 2 days between. Do them after your warm-up, on a sprung court or grass, not concrete. Land softly and quietly, and build up slowly. If your Achilles is sore the next morning, back off. [S16]

A landing tip from one small lab study: after a rear-court forehand jump, rotate your upper body so your non-racket foot lands turned out, like the pros' scissor kick. [S19]

That cut the estimated load on the Achilles by about a quarter. Practise it slowly first, ideally with a coach. [S19]

The common mistake? Stretching instead of strengthening. Stretching programs didn't reduce injuries. Strength training did. [S15]

If your heel or calf hurts, don't play through it. Get it checked by a physio. [S18]

And if you ever feel a sudden snap or a kick to the back of your heel, stop playing straight away and get urgent medical care. [S26]

## Use the gaps
Rest between rallies isn't dead time. It's where the recharger does its job. [S1][S2]

In Olympic finals, the rest between rallies more than doubled over 20 years, from about 15 to over 30 seconds. The researchers think players needed more recovery as the game got faster. [S4]

Habit one: use the time between rallies. Walk back, breathe, reset, and don't rush your serve when you're gassed. Just don't stall: the rules say play must be continuous. [S1][S21]

Habit two: drink in the breaks. Players can lose around a litre of sweat an hour. [S1]

Losing more than 2 to 3% of your body weight in fluid hurts performance. But don't force down more than you sweat. [S20]

Habit three: for long sessions over an hour, like tournaments, have some carbs. A sports drink or a banana. [S20]

## Your week
Here's a simple week. [S14]

Two strength days, at least 2 days apart, with your calf raises and, later, your hops. [S14][S16]

One easy aerobic session, and one interval session. [S11][S14]

One or two short sets of rally repeats before one club night, after a proper warm-up.

Play your club games, and keep one full rest day.

Don't start everything at once. Add one new session every week or two, keep your hard days apart, and skip hops, sprints and intervals the day before a match.

If you're sore or flat, swap a hard session for an easy one. Under 18? Learn the strength and jump drills with a qualified coach first.

Give it 4 to 10 weeks. That's how long training studies took to see changes. [S12]

## Close
A small battery that bursts. A big charger that refills it. Strong legs that carry both.

Train those, use the gaps, and in the third game, you'll be the one who still looks fresh.
