# Research brief: How to never get tired in football

Audience: amateur and club players, 16–40. Every factual line carries [S#], which points to `sources.json`.
Lines marked **(coach-derived)** are practical prescriptions I built from match data plus tested protocols. They are not the exact protocols tested in the studies. The script should present them as "a good starting point", not as "studies show".

**Big honesty note for the whole episode:** almost all football match data comes from **professional** men [S1][S2][S17]. Amateur 11-a-side data is thin. Where the script talks about the viewer's own match, use framing like "even pros…" or "in studies of top players…". The best amateur-level evidence covers recreational small-sided games [S14][S15], an amateur Nordic-hamstring RCT [S27], and the FIFA 11+ trials [S28].

---

## Promise
Still pressing, sprinting back and winning duels in the last 15 minutes. You get there by building the engine football actually runs on: a big aerobic "recharger", a repeat-sprint battery, smart running off the ball and a full fuel tank.

### Does "aerobic base + repeat sprints" hold up? Yes, it's the expert consensus
- Football is mostly low intensity (**>70% of the game** is walking, jogging and standing), yet average oxygen use is **~70% of VO2max** and average heart rate is **~85% of max** [S2][S3][S21]. That combination means a big aerobic load punctuated by **150–250 brief intense actions** [S3].
- Repeated-sprint experts recommend training **both** single-sprint ability and high-intensity intervals (~80–90% VO2max) "to improve the ability to recover between sprints" [S12].
- Aerobic fitness is linked to faster phosphocreatine (sprint fuel) regeneration and better power recovery across repeated efforts [S10].
- The test that best tracks how much high-intensity running a pro does in a match is the **Yo-Yo intermittent recovery test** (r = 0.71). Plain VO2max did not correlate [S6]. So "aerobic" alone isn't the whole story. You need aerobic fitness that shows up *in stop-start running*.

## Part 1: The Recharger (aerobic engine)

**Explanation.** Most of a match is walking and jogging (>70% low intensity) [S2][S3]. Your heart still averages about 85% of max and rarely drops below ~65% [S2]. That's because your aerobic system is working hard between the bursts to refill sprint fuel. Phosphocreatine is only back to ~75% of resting levels 15–30 s after an intense effort [S2]. Fitter players regenerate phosphocreatine faster and keep their power across repeated sprints [S10]. When elite junior players added aerobic intervals, they ran **20% further** in matches, made **twice as many sprints**, and had **24% more ball involvements** [S8].

**Analogy.** A phone charger. Each sprint drains your phone, and the walk back is it on charge. A weak charger means you start every sprint at 70%, then 50%… A fast charger means you're back near full before the next ball over the top.

**Sport moments (mascot-actable).**
- Jog → walk → jog while a battery icon refills (`gauges` / battery)
- 80th minute: an unfit mascot is still walking back when the counter-attack starts, while a fit mascot is already jogging into shape
- Heart-rate needle hovering in the 80s % for the whole match [S2][S21]

**Training methods + dosage.**
1. **4×4 "Norwegian" intervals.** 4 × 4 min running at **90–95% HRmax** (hard, you can say only a few words), **3 min easy jog** between, **2×/week**. This is exactly Helgerud's protocol, which ran 8 weeks in-season [S8]. *Amateur entry (coach-derived):* weeks 1–2 do 3 × 4 min at RPE ~15–16 (hard but controlled; ACSM vigorous range 14–17 [S22]), then build to 4 × 4 at 90–95% HRmax. Do it once a week if you also play a match and train with your team.
   - Ball variant: dribbling a ball round a track gave similar VO2max gains in youth players (McMillan et al. 2005). I didn't open it, so it's in **Unverified**. Use it only as an optional mention.
2. **Small-sided games as intervals.** E.g., **3v3 or 4v4, 4 × 4 min, 2–3 min rest** **(coach-derived format)**. Fewer players and more space per player make the game harder, and coach encouragement raises intensity too [S13]. Small games can match running-based HIIT for intensity and fitness gains [S13]. Recreational small-sided football runs at **80–85% HRmax**, similar to elite matches [S14].
3. **Easy aerobic base.** Jog, cycle or swim at "can still talk" effort (RPE 12–13), **30–45 min, 1×/week**, especially pre-season. This sits inside ACSM's moderate range of 30–60 min [S22]. Progress duration before intensity [S22].
- **Evidence strength:** a meta-analysis of 33 studies found HIIT improved VO2max and repeated-sprint ability in male players **regardless of HIIT type** [S16]. That makes it good evidence that "some form of hard intervals works", so the format can be whatever you'll actually do. The Helgerud numbers come from one small RCT (19 elite juniors) [S8], so present them as "one famous study".

**Not suitable / caution:** hard intervals are for healthy players. If you have chest pain, fainting, unusual breathlessness or a known heart, metabolic or kidney condition, get medical clearance before vigorous training [S30]. Returning from injury: get the physio's OK first.

**Common mistake.** "I'll just do long slow runs." Long slow runs help the base [S22], but the Yo-Yo intermittent test predicts match high-intensity running and VO2max doesn't [S6]. You need *hard, stop-start* aerobic work as well [S12][S16]. The opposite mistake is "just play more matches". Amateurs in small-sided games hit similar heart rates to pros but do less sprinting and high-intensity running [S15], so games alone may not give you enough sprint work.

## Part 2: The Repeat-Sprint Battery

**Explanation.** Elite players make about **33 sprints per match** (≈ one every 3 minutes, up to 68 in some games) [S17]. They cover roughly **0.6–1 km at high speed and 150–300 m sprinting** [S17], and make **150–250 short intense actions** including turns, jumps and tackles [S3]. Top-class pros differ from lower-level pros mainly here: **28% more high-intensity running and 58% more sprinting** [S1]. Pros now sprint much more than they used to. In the Premier League, sprint counts rose from ~31 to ~57 per match between 2006 and 2013, while total distance barely changed (~10.7–10.9 km) [S21]. Repeated-sprint fatigue shows up as each sprint getting slower. It's caused by limits in fuel supply (phosphocreatine, glycolysis, aerobic), metabolite build-up and the nervous system [S11].

**Analogy.** A camera flash. Each sprint is a flash: a huge burst, then it needs a recharge. Part 1 decides how fast it recharges. This part makes the flash brighter and lets it fire more times.

**Sport moments (mascot-actable).**
- Pressing run: sprint at the defender, curve the run, then sprint again for the second ball (`sprint` cycle → `point`)
- Recovery run after losing the ball, then the overlap 20 seconds later
- `hands_knees` after three sprints in a minute (the "temporary fatigue" moment) [S1][S5]

**Training methods + dosage.**
1. **Sprint quality (fresh legs).** **6–8 × 20–30 m** max sprints with a **walk-back recovery (~60–90 s)**, **1–2×/week**, early in the session after a full warm-up **(coach-derived)**. Experts recommend including single-sprint training because a faster top sprint makes each repeat cost less [S12].
2. **Repeated-sprint sets.** Pros used **12–15 × 40 m all-out with 30 s rest** (Dupont) and improved 40 m sprint time by 3.5% over 10 weeks [S9]. *Amateur version (coach-derived):* **2–3 sets × 6 × 20–30 m, 25–30 s rest between reps, 3–4 min between sets**, 1×/week. Stop the set if your times clearly drop off or form breaks.
3. **15:15 intermittent runs (Yo-Yo-style).** Pros did **12–15 × 15 s at 120% of maximal aerobic speed / 15 s rest**, and maximal aerobic speed rose **8.1%** [S9]. *Amateur version (coach-derived):* 15 s fast stride (about 80–90% of sprint effort, not all-out) / 15 s walk, **8–10 min × 2 blocks, 3 min apart**, 1×/week. You can swap this with the 4×4 on alternate weeks.
- Expert view: there's no single "best" method. Combine sprint/power work with intervals at ~80–90% VO2max [S12]. Whether gains in repeated-sprint tests transfer to matches is still unclear [S12].

**Hamstring armour (put it in this chapter).** Hamstring strains are about **12% of all injuries** in pro football, **57% happen while running**, and they **increase toward the end of each half** [S23]. Hamstrings make up 37% of muscle injuries [S24].
- **Nordic hamstring curls** (partner holds your ankles while you lower yourself slowly forward). In **Dutch amateur players** (40 teams, 579 men), **25 sessions over 13 weeks** cut hamstring injuries from 0.8 to 0.25 per 1,000 h [S27]. A meta-analysis found programmes including Nordics roughly **halved** hamstring injuries (RR 0.49) [S25]. A methods reappraisal called the overall effect **inconclusive** (RR 0.59, CI 0.27–1.29) [S26]. **Script line:** "promising, not magic". Dosage per session wasn't in the abstracts. Start with very few slow reps (e.g., 2 × 5) 1–2×/week and build gradually **(coach-derived)**. Expect soreness at first.
- **FIFA 11+ warm-up** (running, strength, balance and jumping drills, ~20 min): **~39% fewer injuries** across cluster RCTs (IRR 0.61) [S28]. This is the single most evidence-backed habit for amateur teams.

**Not suitable / caution:** no max sprints if you have current hamstring, groin or calf pain, or are returning from a strain: get physio clearance. Always do a full warm-up first. Don't do max sprints when you're already exhausted, since that's when hamstring strains cluster [S23] **(placement advice coach-derived)**. Add sprint volume gradually, because rapid jumps in load are a major injury risk [S29].

**Common mistake.** Turning sprint drills into a puke session: short rests, getting slower every rep, sloppy form. That's a different session (Part 1 work). Sprint quality needs real rest [S11][S12]. Another mistake is never sprinting in training at all. Sprint exposure in training is often low compared with matches, even in pros [S17].

## Part 3: Smart Running (spending energy off the ball)

**Explanation.** Fatigue in football comes in two kinds:
- **Temporary:** in the 5 minutes after a player's most intense spell, high-intensity running falls **12% below their match average** [S1] (8% in another dataset [S18]). Sprint times get slower after intense periods in both halves [S5]. It recovers. It's linked to disturbed muscle ion balance, not glycogen or lactate [S4].
- **End-of-match:** high-intensity running is **35–45% lower in the last 15 min than the first 15**, at every level and position [S1]. Substitutes coming on ran **25% more** high-intensity running in that period [S1].

Elite players with very high first-half workrates showed a **12% drop** in second-half high-intensity running, while moderate-workrate players didn't drop [S18]. Running also changes with the score: centre-backs ran less and attackers more when winning big [S18]. In heat, World Cup players ran **less at high intensity** (24.8 vs 26.9 m/min) but kept total distance and *improved* pass accuracy [S19]. Researchers still debate how much of these drops are true fatigue, how much is pacing (conscious or subconscious), and how much is tactics [S2][S18].

**Analogy.** A phone's battery-saver mode. It doesn't make your phone worse; it stops apps you're not using from draining it. Smart players don't stop working. They stop *wasting*.

**Sport moments (mascot-actable).**
- Ball on the far side → mascot walks or jogs into position, scanning (`walk` cycle, head turn)
- Ball turned over nearby → explodes into the press (`sprint`)
- "Pressing trigger" lightbulb (`point_up`): press on a bad touch or a back-pass, not every time

**Habits (coach-derived; the evidence shows pacing *happens*, not that teaching it improves outcomes).**
1. **Recover in the gaps.** When the ball is far away, walk or jog into position instead of drifting at a half-sprint. Use stoppages (throw-ins, goal kicks, injuries) to breathe and reset. That's when the recharger works [S2][S10].
2. **Press with triggers, as a unit.** Agree team pressing cues so you sprint when the press can win the ball. Individual high workrate in the first half is linked to bigger second-half drops [S18].
3. **Adjust in the heat.** Expect less high-intensity running. Prioritise position and passing, as World Cup players naturally did [S19].
4. **Subs are a fitness tool.** In amateur leagues with rolling subs, fresh legs matter late: substitutes ran 25% more high-intensity in the final 15 [S1].

**Common mistake.** "Never stop running" as a badge of honour. All-action running early can cost you late [S18]. The other mistake is using "pacing" as an excuse to switch off. Top-class players still do far more high-intensity running than lower-level ones [S1].

## Part 4: The Fuel Tank (glycogen + fluids)

**Explanation.** Carbohydrate supplies **60–70%** of match energy [S21]. Muscle glycogen is the most important fuel [S3]. After a match, **~47%** of thigh muscle fibres were empty or nearly empty of glycogen in one study [S5], and ~50% after a friendly between lower-division Danish players [S21]. Players who start a game with low glycogen cover less distance and **much less high-speed running, especially in the second half** [S21]. Glycogen depletion in individual fibres is the leading explanation for late-game fatigue [S3][S4][S5]. Heat adds dehydration and reduced brain function as extra factors [S4], and core temperature reaches 39–40 °C in matches [S2].

**Analogy.** A car's fuel tank. You can have a great engine (Parts 1–2), but if you start with half a tank you'll be coasting at 75 minutes.

**Sport moments (mascot-actable).** Pasta or rice meal the night before (fuel gauge filling). A meal 3–4 h before kick-off. A sports drink or banana at half-time. A water bottle at a stoppage on a hot day.

**What the UEFA expert statement says (elite players; adapt sensibly) [S21]:**
1. **Day before:** carbohydrate-focused eating, at least **6–8 g/kg body mass** of carbs (≈ 450–600 g for a 75 kg player, which is a lot; amateurs: "make carbs the main part of every meal").
2. **Pre-match meal:** **1–3 g/kg carbs, 3–4 h before kick-off**, easily digestible and familiar.
3. **Fluids before:** **5–7 ml/kg** in the 2–4 h before kick-off (≈ 400–500 ml for 75 kg), aiming for pale-yellow urine.
4. **During:** ~**30–60 g carbs** after the warm-up and again at **half-time** (e.g., 500 ml sports drink ≈ 30 g; a banana ≈ 25 g — *food carb values are general knowledge, not from S21; fact-checker may want a source or drop them*). Studies show benefits for dribbling, shooting and passing. Effects on sprinting and jumping are less consistent [S21].
5. **Hydration:** sweat rates run **0.5–2.5 L/h** [S21]. Drink enough to avoid losing more than **2–3% of body mass**, but don't drink so much that you gain weight [S21].
6. **Heat:** acclimatise over **1–2 weeks** of training in the heat, and start hydrated [S20].
- Evidence note: UEFA says much football-nutrition research used **recreational-level players** in simulated games, which is useful for our audience [S21]. The statement is a narrative expert consensus, not a systematic review [S21].
- **No supplement claims.** UEFA is "food first" [S21].

**Common mistake.** Skipping carbs before an evening match or playing on an empty stomach after a morning fast. Liver glycogen can drop by ~50% overnight [S21]. The other mistake is chugging litres "just in case". Overdrinking is also flagged [S21].

## Myths

1. **"Football fitness = long slow runs."** The game is stop-start, and match high-intensity running tracks an intermittent test (Yo-Yo), not VO2max [S6]. Experts recommend sprint work plus hard intervals [S12][S16].
2. **"Tired legs at 80 minutes = lactic acid."** Blood lactate is moderate (~5–6 mM) and doesn't track muscle lactate well [S5]. Late fatigue is mostly linked to glycogen running out in muscle fibres [S3][S4][S5]. The short dips after intense spells look more like muscle ion disturbances [S4].
3. **"Good players never stop running."** Top players run more at high intensity [S1], but running rises and falls with the game, the score and the heat [S18][S19]. Running hard when it doesn't matter costs you later [S18].
4. **"Small-sided games aren't real fitness."** They can match running HIIT for intensity and fitness gains [S13], and recreational small-sided football runs at 80–85% HRmax [S14]. Caveat: they may give less sprinting [S15], so add sprints.
5. **"You can drink your way out of fatigue."** Avoid >2–3% dehydration, but overdrinking is flagged too [S21]. Carbohydrate is the bigger late-game lever [S21].
6. **"Nordics are injury-proof armour."** They're promising. One amateur RCT showed big reductions [S27], but a reappraisal calls the overall evidence inconclusive [S26]. Use a full warm-up programme like FIFA 11+ as well [S28].
7. **"Pros run 13 km so I should too."** Distance hasn't changed much. What separates levels is high-speed running and sprinting [S1][S21].

## Safety (for the script and the fact-checker)

- **General:** educational content for healthy amateurs, not medical advice. Medical clearance depends on your current activity level, any symptoms or known heart, metabolic or kidney disease, and how hard you plan to train [S30]. Exercise-related cardiac events are often preceded by warning signs [S30]. **Script line:** "Chest pain, fainting, or unusual breathlessness? Stop and see a doctor before hard training." (ACSM framing [S30]; the specific symptom list is general guidance.)
- **Progression:** build intensity, duration and frequency gradually [S22]. Rapid spikes in load are a major injury risk [S29]. Beginners: 2–3 weeks of base and small-sided games before all-out sprint sets **(coach-derived)**. Allow 8–10 weeks for results [S8][S9].
- **Sprinting / hamstrings:** always warm up. FIFA 11+ cuts injuries ~39% [S28]. Hamstring strains cluster late in each half [S23], so don't do max sprints when exhausted, and build Nordics slowly (expect DOMS) [S27]. **Red flag:** a sudden sharp pain in the back of the thigh mid-sprint means stop, don't "run it off", and get it assessed by a physio **(general clinical advice; not specifically sourced)**.
- **Heat:** acclimatise over 1–2 weeks and start hydrated [S20]. Heat-exhaustion signs: dizziness, headache, nausea, cramps, heavy sweating, intense thirst. Cool down and drink, and you should improve within 30 min [S31]. **Emergency (heatstroke):** confusion, hot skin that isn't sweating, seizure or collapse. Call emergency services [S31].
- **Hydration:** don't exceed a 2–3% body-mass loss, and don't overdrink to the point of gaining weight [S21].
- **Youth (16–17):** sprint, Nordic and interval work should be supervised by a qualified coach. Use bodyweight first **(coach-derived)**.
- **Nutrition:** food-first. No supplement claims [S21]. Carb targets are elite guidelines. Don't present them as mandatory for a recreational kickabout.

## Unverified (do NOT present as fact)

- **Amateur 11-a-side match distances and sprint counts.** I found no peer-reviewed amateur dataset I could open. A university thesis (~9.4 km, ~86% HRmax) and a large consumer-sensor preprint (Footbar, 2025, not peer-reviewed) exist but weren't used. Say "even pros…", or "amateurs typically run less at top speed" [S15].
- **The "12.4% vs 10.4% vs 11.7% high-intensity time (elite / semi-pro / amateur)" study** appears in secondary summaries. I couldn't locate the original, so don't use it.
- **McMillan et al. 2005 (dribbling 4×4 track)** showing VO2max gains: seen only in search snippets.
- **Nordic per-session dose** (sets × reps) in the amateur RCT [S27] isn't in the abstract. The "2 × 5 start" is coach-derived.
- **Off-ball pacing as a trainable skill.** The evidence shows running fluctuates with fatigue, score and heat [S18][S19]. No study shows that teaching amateurs to "pace" improves late-game performance. Habits in Part 3 are coach-derived.
- **Phosphocreatine half-recovery ~30 s / full recovery ~3–5 min** (common textbook figures). Not sourced here. Only "~75% back 15–30 s after an intense effort" [S2] is sourced.
- **Food carb values** (banana ≈ 25 g, 500 ml sports drink ≈ 30 g) are general knowledge. Source or drop.
- **Red-flag wording for hamstring tears and cardiac symptoms** is general clinical guidance, not quoted from a source (ACSM only says warning signs often precede events [S30]).
- **Women's / female-player nutrition and fatigue specifics.** UEFA notes evidence is sparse [S21].
- **Bangsbo GSSI article [S2]** is published by a sports-drink-funded institute. Its numbers match peer-reviewed papers [S1][S3][S21], so the fact-checker may prefer citing those for key stats.
