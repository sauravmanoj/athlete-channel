## The 4 rebound families
In padel, the perimeter walls are not out-of-bounds boundaries; they are active playing surfaces governed by collision physics. [S1][S2]
Every rebound you will face belongs to one of four physical families. [S1][S4]
First is the Single Back Glass, where deep balls rebound forward into the court. [S1][S2]
Second is the Side Glass, where oblique angles compress your lateral hitting space. [S1][S6]
Third is the Double Glass corner, where the ball strikes two walls and either opens into court or traps into your body. [S3][S4]
And fourth is the Metallic Wire Mesh, where compliant metal grids create dead, unpredictable deflections. [S1][S2]
Master these four families, and you will never get jammed off the walls again. [S4][S10]

## Rebound 1: Single back glass
The single back glass is the foundation of all padel defense. [S4]
When a ball crosses the service line, three meters from the back wall, amateurs panic and short-hop it off their shoes. [S1][S8][S10]
Tempered glass follows specular reflection: the angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence. [S1][S2]
An eighty kilometer per hour smash loses thirty-five percent of its speed on the turf bounce and twenty-five percent on the glass. [S2][S6]
It floats forward at thirty to forty kilometers per hour, buying you zero point four to zero point seven seconds of extra reaction time. [S2][S6][S11]
Think of throwing a rubber ball against a brick wall: stand against the brick and it hits your knuckles, but take two steps back and it drops gently into your open hands. [S2]
Execute a sideways unit turn as the ball crosses the net, turning your shoulders perpendicular to the back wall. [S4][S10]
Track backward, stop one to one-and-a-half meters in front of the glass, and let the ball pass your body line. [S4][S5]
As it rebounds forward, transfer your weight and strike thirty to fifty centimeters ahead of your front hip. [S4][S6]
Train this with the Self-Drop Glass Feed: stand one-and-a-half meters from the glass, drop the ball into the wall, and drive the rebound into a target cone. [S4][S10]
Do four sets of twelve reps twice a week on both forehand and backhand sides. [S4]
The biggest mistake is hugging the glass: standing closer than one meter suffocates your arm and ruins your swing. [S5][S10]

## Rebound 2: Side glass angles
The side glass introduces oblique angles that violently compress your lateral hitting space. [S1][S2]
Side glass shots strike at acute angles between thirty and sixty degrees, sliding fast across the court width. [S2][S6]
Amateurs chase the ball toward the side wall, getting their arm pinned directly against the glass. [S4][S10]
Think of a highway exit ramp: steer into the guardrail and you crash, but stay centered in your lane and you follow the curve with total control. [S2]
Maintain an active one-meter buffer from the side wall and track the ball with a stable gaze. [S4][S5][S11]
Stay toward the center, let the ball strike the side glass past your hip, and step into the open court as it rebounds across. [S4][S5]
Hit early to drive down the line, or let it travel twenty centimeters into center court to open a crosscourt lob. [S4][S6]
Train this with the Side Wall Spacing Drill: place three cones one-point-two meters parallel to the side glass. [S4][S5]
Feed angled balls into the glass, stay inside the cones, and drive controlled crosscourt balls. [S4][S5]
Perform four sets of ten reps per side twice a week. [S4]

## Rebound 3: Double glass and the rotation trap
The double glass corner is where amateur players experience total sensory overload. [S3][S4]
In the ninety-degree corner between the back and side glass, the ball strikes two walls consecutively. [S1][S4]
Coaching science divides double glass into two distinct realities: Doble Pared Que Abre, and Doble Pared Que Cierra. [S3][S4]
Doble Pared Que Abre means the double wall opens: the ball hits the side glass first, then the back glass second, rebounding outward toward center court. [S3][S4]
This is the only double wall where you must rotate your body. [S3][S4]
Execute a smooth one-hundred-and-eighty-degree pivot on the balls of your feet, uncoiling your shoulders so your feet face the net as you strike. [S3][S4]
Think of a figure skater: rotate with the ball's orbit, and you emerge facing forward right as the ball presents itself. [S3]
Now look at Doble Pared Que Cierra: the double wall closes. [S3][S4]
The ball hits the back glass first, then the side glass second, trapping inward toward your body. [S3][S4]
If you spin on a closing double wall, you turn your back to the net while the ball dies behind you, increasing unforced errors by over four hundred percent. [S3][S4][S10]
On a closing double wall, do not spin. [S4]
Stay sideways, step forward into the court away from the corner, and let the ball bounce off the side glass directly into your strike zone. [S4]
Think of a dog in a cramped hallway: spinning makes you dizzy, but stepping into the open living room lets the dog run straight to you. [S4]
Practice the Abre versus Cierra Recognition Drill: have a partner feed random corner balls, calling Abre or Cierra before the first impact. [S3][S4][S11]
Do four sets of twelve random feeds, targeting at least ten correct footwork executions per set. [S3][S4]
Beginners should first practice slow shadow footwork on the balls of your feet before full-speed ball drills, wearing herringbone turf shoes to prevent ankle rollover. [S4][S12][S14]
Safety caution: always unweight your feet with a light hop before pivoting; spinning with flat heels on sand turf creates violent twisting torque on knee ligaments and ankles. [S12][S14]

## Rebound 4: The metallic wire mesh
The fourth family is the metallic wire mesh, covering the side walls and upper sections of the court. [S1]
While tempered glass is rigid and predictable, the wire mesh is composed of electro-welded metal wires forming fifty-millimeter grids. [S1][S2]
When a padel ball strikes the mesh, the metal wires flex and vibrate, absorbing tremendous kinetic energy. [S1][S2]
The coefficient of restitution drops from zero point seven-five on glass down to zero point three-five on mesh, causing the ball to drop dead toward the turf. [S1][S2]
If the ball hits a welded wire junction, it deflects upward or sideways at erratic angles of fifteen to forty-five degrees. [S1][S2]
Playing off the mesh requires an ultra-low crouch, flexing your knees so your racket head sits at shin height. [S4][S12]
Eliminate your backswing, hold the paddle out in front, and use a short blocking punch under thirty centimeters to lift the dead ball back into play. [S4][S10]
Think of billiards versus a chain-link fence: glass rolls true like slate, but wire mesh wobbles, absorbs the punch, and drops the ball straight down. [S2]
Train this with the Mesh Drop and React Drill: wearing shatterproof protective eyewear, have a partner hit balls into the side wire fence while you react with a compact blocking scoop. [S4][S7][S11]
Start with two sets of six to eight feeds, progressing to four sets of ten as your legs adapt, and avoid prolonged deep squatting if you have knee pain. [S4][S12]
Never try to blast a winner off the mesh; your only goal is a safe, soft recovery. [S4][S8]

## Safety and the 3-step decision flow
Before taking this blueprint to the court, there are three critical safety rules every player must know. [S7][S13][S15]
First, protect your eyes: an official padel ball measures six-point-five centimeters in diameter, matching the adult eye socket. [S1][S7]
Erratic deflections off the mesh can rocket toward your face with under zero point two-five seconds to react. [S1][S2][S7]
A landmark hospital registry study in Acta Ophthalmologica revealed that padel has become the leading cause of sports-related eye trauma in Sweden, causing severe hyphema and retinal tears. [S7]
Sports ophthalmologists strongly recommend wearing certified shatterproof polycarbonate sports eyewear during close-quarters wall play. [S7]
Second, here is a red flag warning: never back-pedal straight backward blind toward the glass with your heels dragging. [S10][S13]
Blind back-pedaling causes catastrophic backward falls, concussions, and violent collisions with the glass panels. [S13][S14]
Always retreat using sideways crossover steps while keeping your eyes on both the ball and the wall line. [S4][S10]
Third, watch your ankles: sand buildup near the corner walls causes sudden slipping or jamming; if you roll an ankle and cannot bear weight for four steps, see a healthcare professional immediately for an evaluation. [S14][S15]
To pull all four rebound families together, execute this simple three-step mental decision flow on every deep shot. [S4][S5][S11]
Step one: Read depth—if the ball crosses the service line, make the mental commitment to let it go to the glass. [S1][S4]
Step two: Read wall order—if it hits side then back, shout Abre and pivot; if it hits back then side, shout Cierra, step forward, and do not spin. [S3][S4]
And step three: Establish spacing—remember the golden sequence: Wall, Player, Ball. Never let the ball get between your body and the glass. [S4][S10]
