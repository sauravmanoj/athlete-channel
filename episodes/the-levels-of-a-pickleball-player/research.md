# Research brief: The levels of a pickleball player

Audience: amateur and club athletes, 16–40. Every factual line carries [S#] → `sources.json`.
Lines marked **(coach-derived)** are practical prescriptions I built from the official skill descriptors and general training evidence. No study has tested them as pickleball drills, so the script should present them as "a good way to practise this", not "studies show".

**Big honesty notes for the writer:**
1. **Skill level ≠ DUPR rating.** USA Pickleball says skill levels are "not official ratings" and are based "solely on knowledge and proficiencies". Ratings like DUPR come from match results [S2][S9]. They use similar numbers, but they are different systems. Say "skill level" when describing what a player can do, and "DUPR" only when quoting the distribution.
2. **The DUPR distribution is real but has limits.** It is company data reported by a pickleball news site. It covers DUPR users with a doubles result in the last 12 months, the total number of players isn't given, and the figures are rounded to whole percentages. The publisher itself says it is "not the skill distribution of everyone who plays pickleball" [S11]. Keep the numbers, but always say "of players with a DUPR rating".
3. **Almost all injury data come from emergency departments and older players** (mean age ~64) [S14][S15]. For our 16–40 audience the main injury pattern is twisting/rolling the ankle [S14]. Don't scare young viewers with senior fracture statistics, but use them to explain *why* falls matter.

## Promise
Find out which of the five pickleball levels you're really at, from learning the rules to having no weak spots, and the one skill that moves you up to the next level.

### Does a "5 levels" ladder hold up? Yes, with one caveat
- USA Pickleball's official scale runs in half-points: 1.0, then 2.0/2.5 up to 5.0 and 5.5+ [S1][S2]. Its matrix defines 7 rows, from 2.5 Beginner to 5.5+ Expert Pro [S1].
- But USA Pickleball's own overview page **groups the scale into five tiers**: 1.0 · 2.0 & 2.5 · 3.0 & 3.5 · 4.0 & 4.5 · 5.0 & 5.5 [S2]. So "five levels" is how the governing body itself presents it. Use our own level names, not theirs (originality rule).
- The seed ladder (rules → rallies → third-shot drop → resets under pressure → no weak spots) **maps cleanly onto the official descriptors**, but its cut-points differ a little from USA Pickleball's tiers. It splits 3.0 from 3.5 and merges 4.0 with 4.5. I recommend keeping it, because it gives one clear defining skill per level. Frame it as "here's how the official skill chart breaks down", not "there are officially five levels at these exact numbers".
- **One correction to the seed:** in the official matrix the third-shot drop is "developing" at **3.5**, done "with moderate success" at **4.0**, placed "intentionally and consistently" at **4.5**, and "mastered" (from both sides) at **5.0** [S1][S4][S5][S6][S7]. So the drop is the skill that gets you *into* Level 3 and keeps improving after that. It isn't a one-off box you tick.

## Framework: the 5-level ladder (lineup of 5 mascots, each better)

| Level | Name (suggested, ours) | Official range | Defining skill to *reach* the next level | Share of DUPR doubles players* |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The Rookie** | 1.0–2.5 | Know the rules (two-bounce, kitchen), keep score, sustain a short rally [S1][S8] | ~31% rated 2.0–2.99 [S11] |
| 2 | **The Rallier** | ~3.0 | Get serves and returns in (7/10), keep a basic dink rally going, start moving up to the kitchen line [S3] | ~29% at 3.0–3.49 [S11] |
| 3 | **The Dropper** | ~3.5 | **Third-shot drop** "in a way to get to the net", deep serves and returns (8/10) [S1][S4] | ~24% at 3.5–3.99 [S11] |
| 4 | **The Resetter** | 4.0–4.5 | **Block and reset** hard balls into the kitchen, spot "attackable" balls, move with your partner (9/10, then 10/10) [S1][S5][S6] | ~15% at 4.0–4.99 [S11] |
| 5 | **The No-Weak-Spots Player** | 5.0+ | Every shot from **both** forehand and backhand, changes pace at will, "rarely makes unforced errors" (10/10) [S1][S7] | ~1% at 5.0+ [S11] |

\*DUPR ratings are results-based, not the same as USA Pickleball skill levels [S2][S9]. The DUPR population excludes people who aren't rated, and new players start as "NR" (not rated) [S9][S11]. So the true share of all players at Level 1 is probably bigger than 31% (my inference; see Unverified).

**Cumulative version (strong on-screen stat):** of DUPR doubles players, 69% are 3.0+, 40% are 3.5+, **16% are 4.0+**, 5% are 4.5+ and 1% are 5.0+ [S11]. The seed's "~29% at 3.0–3.49" and "~16% at 4.0+" are **confirmed** by this source.

**The ladder's hidden thread (hero beat):** at every step the official sheets raise the same bar: **consistency**. Serve, return and volley "requirements" go **7/10 at 3.0 → 8/10 at 3.5 → 9/10 at 4.0 → 10/10 at 4.5 and 5.0** [S3][S4][S5][S6][S7]. Unforced errors go from "frequent" (3.0) → "significantly reduce game success" (3.5) → "moderate" (4.0) → "limited" (4.5) → "rarely" (5.0) [S1]. Moving up is mostly about missing less, not hitting harder.

**Hero `custom` scene idea:** a staircase of five steps. Each step shows a 10-ball counter filling up (7 → 8 → 9 → 10 → 10) and an unforced-error meter dropping. The mascot climbs one step per chapter.

**Lineup acting:** five mascots left to right. L1 reads a rulebook and serves into the net (facepalm). L2 does a long, steady dink rally. L3 hits a soft arcing drop from the baseline and walks forward to the kitchen line. L4 crouches mid-court and blocks a fast ball softly into the kitchen. L5 hits forehand, backhand, drop and drive with no stumble. The DUPR share appears above each mascot's head.

**Why the soft game matters (rules primer for Level 1–3 scenes):**
- **Two-bounce rule:** the receiver must let the serve bounce, and then the serving side must let the return bounce. Only after that can anyone volley [S8].
- **Non-volley zone ("kitchen"):** 7 ft (2.13 m) from the net on each side, 7 × 20 ft. You can't volley inside it [S8]. Court 20 × 44 ft. Net 34 in at centre, 36 in at the sidelines [S8].
- **Consequence (logic, coach-derived):** the serving team is stuck at the back for the third shot while the returners can already be at the net. That's why a soft third shot that drops into the kitchen is the classic way to get forward. USA Pickleball's 3.5 descriptor literally says "developing the drop shot in a way to get to the net" [S1].
- **Scoring:** in standard scoring only the serving side scores, and games are usually to 11, win by 2. Rally scoring is a provisionally approved option [S8].

## Level 1: The Rookie (≈1.0–2.5): "learn the rules"

**Explanation.** USA Pickleball's 1.0 player has "minimal practical experience, if any" and relies on instructions and rules. The 2.0–2.5 tier can perform fundamentals "with guidance" but lacks consistency [S2]. The official 2.5 line is: "limited experience. Can sustain a short rally with players of equal ability. Basic ability to keep score" [S1]. The jobs at this level are learning the two-bounce rule, the kitchen and the scoring call [S8].

**Analogy.** Learning to drive in an empty car park. Before speed, you need the rules of the road: let it bounce twice, stay out of the kitchen when volleying.

**Sport moments (mascot-actable).** The mascot rushes the net after serving and volleys the return (an illegal two-bounce fault), and a referee whistle blows. The mascot stands in the kitchen to volley, gets a red flash and facepalms. A confused score call ("4-2-…1?") with a question mark above the head.

**What moves you up: get the ball in play.** The 3.0 sheet asks for serves "with depth and direction", "consistently sustain a basic dink rally", and **7 out of 10** on its serve, return and volley checks [S3].

**Training methods + dosage (coach-derived, benchmarks from [S3]).**
1. **10-ball serve test.** Serve 10 from each side (right and left court) and count the ones that land in. Do 3 rounds per session, 2–3 sessions a week. Goal: **7/10** consistently, which is the official 3.0 requirement [S3]. Then aim for the back half of the service box.
2. **10-ball return test.** A partner serves 10 and you return them deep. Same dose. Goal 7/10 [S3].
3. **Dink count.** Both players stand at the kitchen line and dink cross-court, counting the rally. Do 3 × 2 minutes. Goals: 10 in a row, then 20. Every dink must bounce in the kitchen [S8]. (The 3.0 sheet asks you to "consistently sustain a basic dink rally" [S3].)
4. **Rules walk-through.** Play points at walking pace saying "bounce… bounce… now volley" aloud to lock in the two-bounce rule [S8].

**Not suitable / caution:** very low risk. Do a short warm-up first (see Safety). Anyone new to sport after a long break: build up playing time gradually (coach-derived).

**Common mistakes.**
- Swinging hard to "win" points. At this level the official descriptors are about keeping the ball in and keeping score [S1][S3].
- Running in right after the serve and volleying the return, which breaks the two-bounce rule [S8].

## Level 2: The Rallier (≈3.0): "keep the rally alive"

**Explanation.** The 3.0 player hits medium-paced forehands "with some directional intent", avoids the backhand, "can sustain a basic dink rally with limited control", is learning court positioning, and has "frequent unforced errors" [S1]. The 3.0 sheet adds "starting to move to NVZ (non-volley zone) and play there" [S3]. The third shot at this level is "generally a medium paced ball with limited direction" [S1]. In other words, the player just bangs it back.

**Analogy.** Playing catch: the goal is to keep the ball going, not to win the throw.

**Sport moments (mascot-actable).** A long cross-court dink rally with a rally counter climbing. Then the mascot hits a hard third shot from the baseline, the opponents at the net volley it back at its feet, and it facepalms.

**What moves you up: the third-shot drop and deep returns.** The 3.5 sheet asks for serves placed "deep in the court", returns "deep into the court", and a "3rd shot drop/drive to gain advantage to the net with limited success". It also asks for "moves quickly to NVZ when opportunity is there" and understanding "hard game and soft game". Requirements are **8 out of 10** [S4].

**Training methods + dosage.**
1. **Deep-return-and-run.** Your partner serves. You return deep (to the back third, coach-derived target) and then move up to the kitchen line before the ball comes back. Do 3 × 10 reps and track how many returns are deep (goal 8/10 [S4]). Finish each run with a split step (both feet land together, ready). The split step is a coaching standard; see Unverified.
2. **Serve deep 8/10.** Same as the Level 1 test, but count only serves landing in the back half of the box. Goal 8/10 [S4] (coach-derived target zone).
3. **Learn the drop's shape (start close).** Begin with the kitchen-line dink rally, then move back toward the baseline step by step, *keeping the ball soft* as you go (progression described in [S24]; full drill in Level 3).

**Not suitable / caution:** low risk. Move *forward* to the kitchen in control. Don't sprint and lunge (lunging forward was the most common foot/ankle injury mechanism in one clinic series [S17]).

**Common mistakes.**
- Staying at the baseline after returning. The 3.0–3.5 descriptors are explicitly about getting to the NVZ [S3][S4].
- Avoiding the backhand. This is officially a 3.0 trait ("will avoid if possible" persists into 3.5) [S1], so it's a weakness to train now, not later.

## Level 3: The Dropper (≈3.5): "the third-shot drop"

**Explanation.** At 3.5 the player is "developing the drop shot in a way to get to the net", has dinks with "increased consistency" while learning to control height and depth, and "moves quickly towards the NVZ when opportunity is there". Unforced errors "significantly reduce game success" [S1]. The 3.5 checklist describes the drop/drive as working "with limited success" [S4], so a 3.5 player tries it but misses often. Why it matters: because of the two-bounce rule, the serving team must hit the third shot from the back while the returners may already be at the net [S8]. A soft ball that lands in the kitchen forces them to hit up, which buys time to move forward (tactical logic in [S24]).

**Analogy.** A parachute. The ball floats down gently into the kitchen, and while it's in the air you walk forward. A hard drive is a rocket: fast, but it comes straight back at you.

**Sport moments (mascot-actable).** A side-view arc: the ball rises to its peak on your own side, then drops softly over the net into the kitchen. Meanwhile the mascot jogs from the baseline to the line, arriving with a split step. Contrast scene: a drive gets blasted back at the mascot's feet, and an "ouch" pose.

**What moves you up: resets and recognising attackable balls.** The 4.0 sheet asks you to "block and return fast, hard volleys", to be "starting to understand and execute resets", to understand "which balls are attackable in a dink rally", and to show patience to elicit a put-away. The baseline drop/drive now works "with moderate success", with **9/10** requirements [S5].

**Training methods + dosage.**
1. **Drop ladder (step-back drill).** Paraphrased from a coaching manual [S24]. Both players stand just behind the kitchen line and hold an 8-dink rally. Then one player steps back about 2 steps and keeps hitting soft shots into the kitchen until 4 land in. Step back again, and keep going until they can do it from the baseline. Swap after 5 minutes. Cue: lengthen the swing slightly but don't overhit [S24]. **Dose (coach-derived):** 2 × 5 min each role, 2–3×/week.
2. **"Third shot must be a drop" game.** Play normal doubles, but every third shot has to be an attempted drop, even if the point is lost [S24]. **Dose (coach-derived):** one game to 11 per session.
3. **Drop-and-follow.** Hit the drop and take 2–3 steps forward, stopping (split step) when the opponent hits. If the drop was short and soft, keep moving in. If it sat up high, stop and get ready to defend (coach-derived; links to the 3.5 skill "learning to follow ball and minimize coverage gaps" [S1]). Do 3 × 10.
4. **Mix it up once the drop works.** Practise in *random* order (coach or partner feeds a mix of deep and short balls) rather than 50 identical drops. Across 34 studies, random practice transferred better than blocked practice (SMD ~0.40–0.55). But the evidence is mostly low quality, and the effect was smaller and not significant in field studies [S23]. Start blocked to learn the shape, then mix (coach-derived).

**Not suitable / caution:** low risk. Don't backpedal to chase a lob or a deep ball during these games; turn and run instead (see Safety) [S20].

**Common mistakes.**
- Treating the drop as the only option. Even the 4.0 descriptor says players "selectively mix up soft shots with power shots", and 5.0 players "drop and drive ball from both the forehand and backhand side" [S1][S7].
- Hitting the drop and admiring it from the baseline. The point of the drop is to *get to the net* [S1].
- Getting impatient in dink rallies. "Developing patience" and knowing "which balls are attackable" are 4.0 skills [S1][S5].

## Level 4: The Resetter (≈4.0–4.5): "reset under pressure"

**Explanation.** The 4.0 player is "starting to understand the block/re-set volley" and "beginning to understand and execute resets". They're aware of their partner's position, have a "moderate number of unforced errors", and are "beginning to identify opponents' weaknesses" [S1]. At 4.5 they can "block hard volleys directed at them and consistently drop them into the NVZ", "intentionally and consistently place the 3rd shot drop", have "good footwork and move laterally, backward, and forward well", and have a "limited number of unforced errors" [S1][S6]. A reset is a soft block that takes the pace off a hard ball and drops it into the kitchen. It turns defence back into a neutral dink rally (definition paraphrased from [S1][S6] wording "block… drop them into the NVZ").

**Why this is the big step for real players:** in professional men's doubles, **71.3% of shots were played from the transition zone** (mid-court, between the baseline and the kitchen). Most points lasted only **3–9 seconds** [S13]. Even the best players are often caught mid-court, so the skill of absorbing pace and resetting decides a lot of points (inference from [S13]; it's elite data, so don't overstate it for amateurs).

**Analogy.** Catching an egg. Give with the hands and soak up the speed. Or a "pause button": the rally is going too fast, so you press pause and start a calm dink exchange again.

**Sport moments (mascot-actable).** The mascot is caught mid-court in a low, wide stance, and an opponent drives hard at its feet. A short "soft hands" block pops the ball gently into the kitchen, and the action slows down. Partner mascots shift left/right together like they're tied by a rope ("moves as a team" [S5]).

**What moves you up: no weak side, no free points.** At 5.0 every shot type is "at a high level… from both the forehand and backhand sides including touch, spin, and pace". The player is "successful at turning defensive shots into offensive shots" and "rarely makes unforced errors", with **10/10** requirements (already 10/10 at 4.5) [S1][S6][S7].

**Training methods + dosage (coach-derived, skills from [S5][S6]).**
1. **Transition-zone reset drill.** You stand mid-court and your partner stands at the kitchen line feeding medium-paced balls at your feet and hip. You block them softly so they land in the kitchen. Do **3 sets × 10 balls**, forehand and backhand, with 1 min rest between sets. Progressions: (a) a slower feed with a target cone in the kitchen → (b) faster feeds → (c) after each successful reset, step forward and play the point out live. Goal: 9/10 into the kitchen (borrowing the 4.0 9/10 benchmark [S5]; the sheet's 9/10 applies to volleys).
2. **"Attackable ball" dink game.** Dink cross-court, and you may only speed the ball up if it bounces up above net height. Otherwise you must dink. Play to 11 (coach-derived rule; trains "understands which balls are attackable" [S5]).
3. **Block wall (solo).** Stand about 2–3 m from a practice wall and absorb rebounds softly with short punches and blocks. Do 3 × 1 min (coach-derived). Wear eye protection [S19].
4. **Partner movement shadowing.** Without a ball, two partners slide left/right and forward/back together on a coach's point. Do 4 × 30 s (coach-derived; "moves as a team" [S5]).

**Not suitable / caution:** fast hands-battle drills put the ball near the face. Wear sports eyewear (see Safety) [S18][S19]. Keep feeds at a pace the receiver can control, and progress gradually.

**Common mistakes.**
- Swinging at every hard ball. The 4.0–4.5 skill is to *block* and drop it, not counter-hit everything [S1][S6].
- Speeding up "unattackable" low balls. Recognising which balls are attackable is an official 4.0 benchmark [S5].
- Ignoring your partner. Moving as a team and stacking appear from 3.5 to 4.5 [S1][S5][S6].

## Level 5: The No-Weak-Spots Player (≈5.0+)

**Explanation.** The 5.0 Expert has "mastered the dink and drop shots", can "move opponents with shot placements", "changes pace of play easily using drives, drops, blocks, dinks", uses "surprise and deception", has "efficient footwork", adjusts the game plan to opponents and partner, and "rarely makes unforced errors" [S1][S7]. The 5.5+ tier is a "top caliber player" whose "performance and tournament wins speak for" their ability [S1]. Athleticism (mobility, quickness, hand-eye coordination, previous sports experience) is factored into every assessment [S3][S7]. Only about **1%** of DUPR doubles players are rated 5.0+ [S11].

**Analogy.** A castle with no weak wall: whichever side you attack, it holds. Or a chess player with every piece developed.

**Sport moments (mascot-actable).** A montage: forehand drop, backhand drop, drive, lob, dink, overhead. Then a "weakness radar" chart with every spoke full. The "1%" stat drops in.

**Training methods + dosage (coach-derived).**
1. **Weak-side week.** Spend 10 minutes per session playing **backhand-only** dinks and drops. Even 4.0 asks for only "somewhat consistent" backhands, while 5.0 asks for both sides at a high level [S5][S7]. 3 sessions/week.
2. **The official 10/10 tests.** 10 serves, 10 returns and 10 volleys, and you count them. 10/10 is the 4.5/5.0 requirement [S6][S7]. Retest monthly.
3. **Unforced-error tally.** Have a friend tick every unforced error in one game, exactly as the official rating sheet does ("make tick mark for every UE") [S3][S7]. Train the shot that produces the most ticks.
4. **Random-feed scenarios.** A coach feeds drive / drop / lob / dink in a random order and you choose the right reply. Do 3 × 2 min [S23] (format coach-derived).
5. **Athletic base (all levels, especially 4–5).** Do strength training for all major muscle groups **at least 2 days/week**, building gradually. For strength, ~80% 1RM for 2–3 sets per exercise; bands and bodyweight work too [S22]. Strength training cut sports injuries to under a third in RCTs (RR 0.315), and balance/proprioception training reduced them too (RR 0.55). These are not pickleball-specific [S21]. Suggested menu (coach-derived): split squats, lateral lunges, calf raises (straight and bent knee), hip hinge, rows, and single-leg balance with a head turn.

**Not suitable / caution:** strength work should start light for anyone new to lifting [S22]. Anyone with current calf, Achilles, ankle or shoulder pain should get it checked before adding load (general caution).

**Common mistakes.**
- Chasing power. The top level is defined by control, variety and "rarely" missing, not by pace alone [S1][S7].
- Only playing rec games. Ratings and the DUPR reliability score improve with more matches, recency and *varied* partners and opponents [S9][S10].

## Myths

1. **"My DUPR is my skill level."** USA Pickleball skill levels describe what you can do, while DUPR comes from match results [S2][S9]. DUPR can even go *up* after a loss or *down* after a win, depending on whether you beat the expected score [S10].
2. **"To move up, hit harder."** The official ladder raises consistency (7/10 → 10/10) and cuts unforced errors at every step. The soft game (dinks, drops, resets) is central from 3.0 onward [S1][S3]–[S7].
3. **"The third shot must always be a drop."** 4.0 players "selectively mix up soft shots with power shots", and 5.0 players "drop and drive… from both sides" [S1][S7]. The drop is a tool, not a law.
4. **"Most players are 4.0+."** Only about 16% of DUPR doubles players are 4.0 or higher, and ~1% are 5.0+. The single biggest band is 3.0–3.49 (~29%) [S11].
5. **"Pickleball is too gentle to get hurt."** In the US, ED-treated injuries rose 91% between 2020 and 2022 [S15]. Achilles ruptures were the top foot/ankle diagnosis in one clinic series [S17]. Young adults most often twist or roll an ankle [S14].
6. **"Stretching before play prevents injuries."** In RCT pooling, stretching showed no effect (RR 0.96), while strength training (RR 0.32) and balance training (RR 0.55) did [S21]. Keep a warm-up, but don't rely on static stretching.
7. **"Backpedal to chase a lob."** Expert opinion is to turn and run toward where the ball will land. Backpedalling while looking up puts your weight behind you and leads to backward falls [S20]. Falls are the leading cause of injury overall [S14][S16].
8. **"Normal glasses protect your eyes."** There's a specific standard for racket-sport eye protectors that names pickleball (ASTM F3164-24), and it recommends polycarbonate lenses [S19].

## Safety (for the script and the fact-checker)

- **General:** this is education for healthy amateurs, not medical advice. Anyone with current pain should see a physio or sports doctor before adding training load.
- **Falls are the #1 mechanism** of ED-treated pickleball injuries (65.5%) [S14], and 92% of fractures come from falls [S16]. Wrist is the most injured site [S14]. Most of these are in older players, but the lesson applies to everyone.
  - **Lobs:** turn and run, don't backpedal (expert opinion [S20]). If you can't reach it, let it go.
  - **Court:** keep stray balls off the court (a common coaching rule; not sourced, so present it as a tip).
- **Achilles and calf:** in one clinic series, Achilles rupture was the most common foot/ankle diagnosis (39.4%) and calf injury was next (15.7%). The top mechanism was **running or lunging forward** (30.9%) [S17]. Patients were mostly middle-aged and older (mean 58) [S17]. Safe framing: "explosive forward lunges load the Achilles and calf. Warm them up and strengthen them." Calf raises are a reasonable strength exercise (coach-derived), and strength training broadly reduces sports injuries [S21][S22].
- **Ankle sprains for 16–40s:** twisting/inversion is the most common mechanism at 18–34 [S14]. Balance/proprioception training lowered injury risk in RCTs (RR 0.55, not pickleball-specific) [S21]. Wear court shoes rather than running shoes (authors' recommendation of "proper footwear" [S15]; specifics unverified).
- **Eyes:** a ball strike is the most common cause of eye injury (43%) [S18]. For hands battles and wall drills, recommend sports eyewear that meets **ASTM F3164** with polycarbonate lenses [S19]. No level of play currently mandates eye protection (reported in a PMC review I could not open; see Unverified).
- **Warm-up:** the NEISS authors recommend an adequate warm-up, footwear and resistance training [S15], but no trial has tested a pickleball warm-up. Suggested routine **(coach-derived)**: 5–8 minutes of light jogging, side shuffles, carioca, leg swings, walking lunges, calf pogo hops (low), arm circles, then 3–5 minutes of soft dinks building up to drives.
- **Red flags → stop and see a professional (general caution, keep it non-diagnostic):**
  - Sudden sharp pain or a "kick" sensation at the back of the heel or calf, or you can't push off or rise on your toes.
  - Any eye hit followed by blurred vision, flashes, floaters or pain [S18 lists retinal detachment and globe trauma as possible injuries].
  - A fall onto an outstretched hand with swelling or deformity at the wrist [S14][S16].
  - Hitting your head in a fall.
  - Ankle "rolls" that leave you unable to bear weight.
- **Progression:** new players should build session length gradually, and all hard-hitting drills should start at slow feeds (coach-derived).
- **No supplements, braces as treatment, or medical treatments** are recommended.

## Unverified (do NOT present as fact)

- **Share of *all* players at each level.** The DUPR numbers cover only rated DUPR users with a recent doubles result [S11]. Casual players (most of the 24.3 million [S25]) are mostly unrated, so Level 1's real share is very likely bigger. That's my inference, not data.
- **Mapping DUPR numbers onto USA Pickleball skill levels.** The two use similar numbers but different methods [S2][S9]. I found no official conversion. Present the DUPR shares as "of players with a DUPR rating, X% are rated 3.0–3.49", not "X% of players are 3.0-skill".
- **Third-shot drop vs drive success rates** (e.g. "66% drops, 45% win rate" from blogs analysing pro matches) and **"serving team loses 65% when it fails to reach the kitchen"**. Both come from blogs or analytics sites with unclear samples. Not used.
- **"90.8% of shots were intercepted"** [S13]. I only read the abstract, and "intercepted" probably means volleyed or taken before a second bounce, but the definition wasn't confirmed. Don't use it on screen.
- **Pickleball Achilles study (43 of 2,684; mean age 64.5; Foot & Ankle Specialist)**. Seen only via a search snippet and a proxy mirror. Not cited.
- **"No level of play mandates eye protection"** and the AAO's endorsement of ASTM F3164. Reported in a PMC review and news pieces that I couldn't open. The ASTM standard itself [S19] is confirmed.
- **JAMA Ophthalmology eye study** numbers come via The Dink's summary [S18], not the paper itself.
- **Split step, "turn and run", court shoes vs running shoes, stray-ball rule.** Standard coaching advice. Only "turn and run" has a source, and it's expert opinion [S20].
- **All drill dosages** (sets × reps, minutes, frequency) and the "attackable above net height" rule are coach-derived. The only drill taken from coaching material is the drop ladder and the "third shot must be a drop" game [S24]. The benchmarks (7/8/9/10 of 10) are official [S3]–[S7], but those rater sheets don't spell out the exact test protocol (e.g. which targets count).
- **Time to move up a level.** I found no credible data on how long it takes to go from 3.0 to 4.0. Don't give a number.
- **Pickleball-specific warm-up or injury-prevention trials.** None found. Warm-up advice is extrapolated from general sport evidence [S21] and author recommendations [S15].
- **Tennis NTRP / other sports' rating ladders.** Not opened, so they need sourcing before reuse.
