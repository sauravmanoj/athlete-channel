# The levels of a cricket fast bowler
Promise: climb from an arm-only slinger to a fast, repeatable bowler whose back survives the season — one level at a time, with the drill that unlocks each level.
Framework: five levels — Arm Slinger, Rhythm Runner, Aligned Bowler, The Whip, Durable Strike Bowler

## Hook
You charge in, put everything into it, and the ball still floats down.
In one study, club bowlers averaged about 90 kilometres an hour. [S8] Australian first-class bowlers averaged a top speed of about 127. [S7]
Elite fast bowlers usually sit around 130 to 145. [S6] And the very quickest have been clocked above 160. [S31]
That gap isn't just talent. It's a ladder with five levels. Today: where you are, and the drills that move you up.

## The five levels
This is how I group it. It's a coaching ladder, not an official scale. [S2][S4]
Level 1, the Arm Slinger. Level 2, the Rhythm Runner. Level 3, the Aligned Bowler. Level 4, the Whip. And Level 5, the Durable Strike Bowler.
Each level builds on the one before, so it pays to climb in order.

## Level 1: The Arm Slinger
The Arm Slinger walks in and tries to bowl fast with the arm alone.
Across 18 studies, faster bowlers tended to have quicker run-ups and stronger, more powerful bodies. [S1]
It's like throwing a dart with just your wrist. You've left your legs and body out of the job.
Fast bowling is one movement in phases: run-up, gather, back foot lands, front foot lands, release, follow-through. [S4][S6]
And you choose one shape. Side-on, with your back foot parallel to the crease. Or front-on, with your chest more open. Both are fine. [S17]
Before any bowling, running or jumping drill in this video, warm up for 5 to 10 minutes: easy jogging, skips, walking lunges and a few easy run-throughs. [S28]
Drill 1: walk-throughs. 3 walking steps into your action at half effort. 3 sets of 6 balls at a target, filmed on your phone.
Drill 2: stand and deliver. From the back-foot position, step and bowl. 3 sets of 6 at 60 to 70 percent. Front foot lands first, then the arm comes over late. [S2][S3]
The mistake? Muscling the arm harder. The speed lives in the run-up, the brace and the sequence. [S1][S2]

## Level 2: The Rhythm Runner
Level 2 is the run-up. Run-up speed is one of the most consistent predictors of ball speed. [S1][S2][S3]
The fastest part should be the last 5 metres. In one review, speed over that final stretch tracked ball speed closely. [S6]
Think of a motorway on-ramp. Build speed smoothly, and arrive at the crease at the fastest speed you can still control.
Faster isn't always better, though. Your run-up has to match the action you can hold together. [S6][S9]
Drill 1: mark your run-up. Start with 8 to 12 strides, put down a marker, and do 6 to 8 run-throughs without a ball.
Drill 2: build-up runs. 4 to 6 runs of 20 to 25 metres, with the last 5 metres fastest. [S6] Walk back between each.
Drill 3: run-up plus delivery at 70 to 80 percent. 2 to 3 sets of 6 balls, and count every one in your weekly total. [S24]
The mistake? A run-up so long or so fast that you stutter and brake before the crease, throwing away your momentum. [S6][S9]

## Level 3: The Aligned Bowler
Level 3 is the most important level, because this is where you protect your back.
A mixed action means your hips point one way while your shoulders twist round the other way. [S17]
Picture wringing out a wet towel, then slamming it down on your front foot. Your spine is the towel.
That twist is linked to lower back pain and to stress fractures in the low back. [S4][S18]
And in one small study of high-performance bowlers, more twist didn't go with more ball speed. [S5] So the twist seems to buy you risk, not pace.
Then the front leg. In studies of men, the fastest bowlers tend to land on a firm, straighter front knee. [S1][S3][S4] In women, the picture is more mixed. [S9][S10]
It's like a pole vaulter's plant. The leg stops, and the body is catapulted forward. A collapsing knee leaks that energy. [S2]
But that leg takes a big hit. The front foot absorbs roughly 5 to 9 times your body weight on landing. [S6] So you earn the brace with strength. You don't just lock your knee.
Drill 1: the phone check. Film 6 balls from behind and side-on. At back-foot contact, do your feet, hips and shoulders point roughly the same way? [S4][S17] If you see a big twist, get a qualified coach to look. Changing an action is a coach's job. [S16][S17]
Drill 2: step and brace. From 3 steps, land on the front foot, firm the knee, let your chest come over it, and hold 2 seconds. 3 sets of 6, twice a week, without a ball at first.
Drill 3: front-leg strength. Split squats with a slow 3-second lowering. 2 to 3 sets of 8 to 12 per leg, twice a week. [S27][S6]
The mistake? Trying to fix your action alone from video clips. The twist is hard to judge by eye. [S16][S19]

## Level 4: The Whip
At Level 4, power flows through the whole chain. [S2]
The front leg plants, the hips open, the chest drives forward and down, and the arm comes through late. [S3][S4]
It's like cracking a whip. The handle stops sharply, and the energy races down to the tip. Your hand is the tip.
One detail matters. Faster bowlers open up between hips and shoulders late, at front-foot contact, not by twisting early. [S4] So the cue is "late", not "more twist".
And don't lean sideways for pace. Side-bend is linked to back injury. [S19][S20]
Drill 1: plyometrics, the best-supported speed method so far. In a review of small fast-bowling studies, jump training had a moderate effect on bowling speed. [S11]
Start low: about 60 easy foot contacts, like pogo hops, twice a week with 2 days between. Over a few weeks, add bounds and skater jumps, and stay at or under about 100. [S28]
Land softly, on grass or a sprung floor, and not the day before you bowl hard.
Drill 2: a med-ball power circuit. In one small study, club bowlers did med-ball throws, squats and split squats twice a week, plus bowling slightly heavier and lighter balls. They gained about 5 kilometres an hour in 4 weeks. [S8]
If you try modified balls, keep them within about 10 to 15 percent of normal weight, use them with a coach, and count them in your total. [S8][S11]
Under 18, or new to jumping and lifting? Get a coach to supervise. Had back pain or a recent growth spurt? Get checked first. [S23]
The mistake? Thinking the gym alone will do it. Lifting on its own showed only a small, uncertain effect on speed. [S11][S8]

## Level 5: The Durable Strike Bowler
Level 5 is the bowler who's still fast in the last over of the season.
Fast bowlers are the most injured players in cricket. [S16] Stress fractures in the low back cost the most playing time, and they hit young bowlers hardest. [S15][S16]
The biggest trigger is the spike. In one study of elite bowlers, a week of more than double their usual load, meaning their average over the last month, came with 3 to 4 times the injury risk the following week. [S13]
In English county pros, weeks of 234 balls or more, about 39 overs, came with roughly 3 times the risk of a back stress fracture, compared with weeks under about 200. [S15]
Think of your body's load tolerance like a savings account. Steady deposits build the balance. One huge withdrawal bounces, and the bill can arrive a week or more later. [S13]
But resting completely isn't safe either. Bowlers with too little regular bowling were also more likely to get hurt. [S13][S16]
Drill 1: count your balls. Log every hard delivery. Build up gradually, never jump to double your usual week, and borrow the junior rule: a lighter week every 4 to 6 weeks. [S13][S23]
Drill 2: spread your bowling days. Elite bowlers had the lowest injury risk with about 3 to 4 days between bowling sessions. [S13] So a good starting point for adults is 2 bowling days a week, well spaced, and 3 at most.
Under 19? Follow your board's limits. Cricket Australia, for example, caps under-17s at 5-over spells and 3 bowling days a week. [S23][S24]
Drill 3: back armour. An 8-week exercise programme made 14 to 17 year old bowlers stronger, with better back endurance, and slightly reduced the twist without slowing the ball. [S21][S22] It didn't test injuries.
A good starting point: back holds, side planks and single-leg squats, 2 to 3 times a week. Keep it pain-free.
Most important: if you get low back pain while or after bowling, especially on one side, stop bowling and see a sports doctor or physio before you bowl again. These bone injuries can start before the pain does. [S23][S15]

## Put it together
A simple week. Monday: strength and jumps. Tuesday: rest, plus the back circuit. Wednesday: nets, run-up rhythm, 4 to 6 overs. [S27][S28]
Thursday: lighter strength, a few jumps and the back circuit again. Friday: rest. Saturday: match. Sunday: rest. [S23]
And before the season, build up over 6 to 10 weeks. Don't go from zero to full spells. [S23]

## Close
Run in with rhythm, line up, brace, whip it through late, and count every ball.
Climb one level at a time, and you won't just bowl faster. You'll still be bowling fast at the end of the season.
