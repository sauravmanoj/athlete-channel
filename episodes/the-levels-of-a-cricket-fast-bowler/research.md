# Research brief: The levels of a cricket fast bowler

Audience: amateur and club cricketers, 16–40. Every factual line carries [S#] → `sources.json`.
Lines marked **(coach-derived)** are practical prescriptions I built from the evidence plus general training guidelines. They are not tested cricket protocols, so the script should present them as "a good starting point", not "studies show".

**Big honesty note for the writer:** almost all fast-bowling biomechanics is **correlational** and comes from **small elite male samples** [S1][S2][S3]. Say "the fastest bowlers tend to…", not "doing X adds Y km/h". The one exception with real training data is plyometrics and combined strength/power work [S8][S11].

---

## Promise
Climb from an arm-only slinger to a fast, repeatable bowler whose back survives the season, one level at a time, with the drill that unlocks each level.

### Does a "5 levels" ladder hold up?
- Experts describe the action in phases: run-up → gather/bound → **back-foot contact** → **front-foot contact** (the "block") → release → follow-through [S4][S6][S17]. The consensus speed variables map onto those phases: a faster run-up, efficient sequential transfer of linear to angular momentum, quick deceleration on an **extended front knee**, and a **delayed bowling arm** [S2][S3]. The main injury variable is **shoulder counter-rotation / mixed action** between back-foot and front-foot contact [S4][S17][S18]. Workload is the other main injury lever [S13][S15][S16].
- So the ladder below is a **teaching progression** that I built from those phases. It is not a published classification. Present it as "here's how I'd group it", not "scientists define five levels".

## Level 1: The Arm Slinger

**Explanation.** A bowler who walks in and "throws with the arm" leaves most of the available speed unused. Across 18 studies, speed was more strongly related to whole-body physical qualities (r≈0.65) and run-up speed (r≈0.50) than to things like centre-of-mass speed at release or vertical ground force [S1]. Fast bowling is a whole-body skill: the consensus picture is running momentum turned into rotation through a braking front leg, with the arm last [S2][S3]. (Note: in cricket the arm must not straighten at the elbow during delivery under the Laws, so "throwing harder" isn't a legal route. See Unverified for the exact tolerance.)

**Analogy.** Throwing a dart with only your wrist. You can do it, but you've left your legs and body out of the job.

**Sport moments (mascot-actable).** The mascot stands at the crease, no run-up, arm windmills over (`arm_swing`/`throw`). The speed gun shows a low number. Then cut to Level 2.

**What to learn here (the basic shape).**
- The phases: run-up → gather/jump → back-foot lands → front-foot lands → release → follow-through [S4][S6][S17].
- Choose an action that's **consistent**: **side-on** (back foot parallel to the crease, shoulders pointing down the pitch) or **front-on** (back foot pointing down the pitch, chest more open). Both are considered acceptable. The problem is mixing them [S17].

**Training methods (coach-derived).**
1. **Walk-through action drill.** From 3 walking steps: back foot, front foot, release, follow-through at ~50% effort. Do **3 sets × 6 balls**, aiming at a target. Film from behind and side-on with a phone. Under ECB rules, deliberately slow walk-throughs don't count toward junior over limits [S24].
2. **"Stand and deliver" with a step.** Start from the back-foot-contact position, step to the front foot and bowl. Do **3 × 6** at 60–70% effort. Learn the order: front foot lands, *then* the arm comes over (the arm should be late) [S2][S3].
3. **Follow-through finish.** After every rep, let the momentum carry you 4–6 steps past the crease rather than stopping dead. (Common coaching cue; no study tests it, so see Unverified.)

**Not suitable / caution:** anyone with current back or shoulder pain should get it checked first [S23].

**Common mistake.** Trying to get faster by **muscling the arm** harder. The evidence says speed lives in the run-up, the brace and the sequence [S1][S2][S3].

## Level 2: The Rhythm Runner

**Explanation.** Run-up speed is one of the most consistent predictors of ball speed. Meta-analysis gives r≈0.50 [S1], it's a consensus variable across 15 studies [S2], and the fastest elite men had quicker run-ups [S3]. Run-up length correlated r≈0.70 with delivery speed, and run-up speed may account for up to ~16% of release speed [S6]. Typical run-up speeds are ~17–22 km/h, with the highest values (~22 km/h) in the last 5 m. Speed over the final 5 m correlated r≈0.72 with ball speed [S6]. **But faster isn't always better.** Run-up speed must be balanced against rhythm and the action [S6], and in women the link largely tracked height. The authors suggest "individual-specific optimal run-up speeds" [S9].

**Analogy.** A car on a motorway on-ramp. Build speed smoothly so you arrive at the crease at your best controllable speed. Don't sprint off the mark and then brake before the crease.

**Sport moments (mascot-actable).** Mascot run cycle, smoothly accelerating, with tick marks on the ground for the run-up markers. Then a contrast: the mascot sprints, stutters and chops its steps (off-balance pose) before the crease.

**Training methods + dosage (coach-derived).**
1. **Measure and mark your run-up.** Start ~8–12 strides (a mid-length run-up for most club bowlers; coach-derived). Walk it back from the crease and put down a marker. Do **6–8 run-throughs without a ball**, checking that you hit the same marks. Use full recovery (walk back).
2. **Build-up runs.** **4–6 × 20–25 m** accelerating smoothly so the last 5 m are fastest [S6], at ~80–90% effort, walk back. Before bowling, after a warm-up.
3. **Run-up + delivery at 70–80%.** **2–3 sets × 6 balls** concentrating on hitting the crease at the same spot. Count these balls in your weekly total. Under ECB rules, near-match-intensity training balls count toward junior limits [S24].

**Not suitable / caution:** this is mostly running, so it's low risk. Beginners should build the number of near-full-effort balls gradually (see Level 5) [S13][S23].

**Common mistakes.**
- **Run-up too long or too fast to control**, so you decelerate before the crease and lose the momentum you built. Run-up speed has to match the action you can hold [S6][S9].
- **Inconsistent run-up** leads to no-balls and a different action every ball. (Common coaching observation, not tested in the sources.)

## Level 3: The Aligned Bowler (the safe-action level)

This is the **key safety chapter**. Spend the most care on it.

### 3a. Line up hips and shoulders (no "mixed action")
**Explanation.** A **mixed action** means the bowler changes from front-on to side-on during the delivery stride. Typically the back foot and hips point one way (front-on) while the shoulders twist round to side-on. That twist is **shoulder counter-rotation** [S17]. Bowlers with lumbar stress fractures had higher counter-rotation [S4]. A systematic review found **moderate evidence** that counter-rotation (associated with mixed actions) is linked to low back pain [S18]. Studies have used cut-offs from ~20° to ~40° for "too much" counter-rotation [S17][S19]. Lumbar stress fractures in fast bowlers occur mainly at **L4–L5** [S15], and pars (bone) abnormalities show up mainly on the **non-bowling-arm side** (81% of bowlers vs 36% of controls in one MRI study) [S17].

**Nuance the script must keep (evidence strength: moderate, observational):**
- In some elite cohorts, 73–78% of bowlers were classed as "mixed", and the label alone wasn't consistently linked to injury. The *amount* of counter-rotation and side-bend seems to matter more than the label [S19].
- **Counter-rotation doesn't make you faster.** In high-performance bowlers, ball speed had no association with shoulder counter-rotation [S5]. So the twist is all risk and no reward. This is the strongest beat of the chapter.
- A coaching/education programme (6 sessions over 3 years) raised "safe" (non-mixed) technique from 20% to 67% of young bowlers. Only 1 safe-technique bowler developed new disc degeneration, compared with 20 in the mixed group [S17]. But a large surveillance paper notes it **hasn't been shown prospectively** that changing technique reduces stress fractures [S16]. Say "coaches and researchers recommend", not "fixing it prevents fractures".

**Analogy.** Wringing out a wet towel: hips twisted one way, shoulders the other, then the whole thing is slammed down onto the front foot. The spine is the towel.

### 3b. Brace the front leg ("the block")
**Explanation.** The fastest elite men kept a **straighter front knee** through front-foot contact [S3][S4]. Front-knee angle at release correlates r≈0.40 with speed [S1]. "Quick deceleration with an extended front knee" is a consensus variable [S2]. Faster bowlers also produced bigger, quicker braking forces [S4]. **The cost:** the front foot absorbs roughly **5–9× body mass** on landing [S6], and a straighter knee raises shear and ground-reaction forces [S19]. Eccentric (braking) leg strength may help you hold the brace and tolerate the impact [S6].
- **Sex difference:** in one study of elite women, the faster bowlers had *more* knee bend, which the authors linked to strength differences (front knees collapsing) [S10]. Another study of women found a straighter knee related to speed once height was accounted for [S9]. Treat "brace" as a goal you build strength for, not a cue to lock the knee.

**Analogy.** Pole-vault plant, or a runner tripping over a kerb: the legs stop dead and the upper body is catapulted forward. A bent, collapsing front knee is like planting a bendy pole: the energy leaks away.

**Sport moments (mascot-actable).** Back-foot contact freeze-frame, with callouts on the back foot, hips and shoulders all pointing the same way. Then front-foot contact with a callout on the front knee ("firm, not locked"). Versus scene: the mixed-action mascot shows a twisted torso and a red glow on the lower back.

**Training methods + dosage (coach-derived unless cited).**
1. **Phone video check (alignment screen).** Film 6 balls from directly behind and side-on at match pace. At back-foot contact, check whether feet, hips and shoulders point roughly the same way (all side-on or all front-on). Counter-rotation is the variable linked to lumbar stress fractures [S4][S17]. If you can't tell or you see a big twist, **get a qualified coach to look**. Changing an established action is a coach's job [S16][S17].
2. **Step-and-brace drill.** From a 3-step approach, land on the front foot, firm the knee, let the chest come over the front leg, and hold the finish for 2 s. Do **3 × 6 per session**, 2×/week, no ball at first, then with a ball at 60–70%.
3. **Front-leg strength (to earn the brace):** split squats or reverse lunges, then step-downs or eccentric (slow 3-s lowering) split squats. Do **2–3 sets × 8–12 per leg, 2×/week, 48 h apart**, at a moderate-hard load (ACSM ranges [S27]). The aim is eccentric strength for the front-leg block [S6].

**Not suitable / caution:** do not try to straighten a front knee that currently collapses by "locking" it at full pace. Build strength and progress the brace at reduced speed first, because a straighter knee raises impact forces [S6][S19]. Anyone with current back, knee or ankle pain should see a physio first [S23].

**Common mistakes.**
- Thinking the twist (counter-rotation) generates pace. It doesn't [S5], and it's linked to back injury [S4][S18].
- "Fixing" an action alone from YouTube clips. Action changes need a coach, and the injury evidence is about the amount of twist and side-bend, which is hard to judge by eye [S16][S19].

## Level 4: The Whip (speed from the whole chain)

**Explanation.** Once the run-up, alignment and brace are in place, speed comes from **sequencing**. Momentum stops at the front leg and is passed up the body (linear → angular momentum) [S2]. The fastest elite men showed **more upper-trunk flexion** (chest driving forward and down over the front leg) and a **delayed arm** [S3]. Faster bowlers reached **maximum hip–shoulder separation later** in the stride and rotated the shoulders more through to release [S4]. Hip–shoulder separation and lateral trunk flexion were associated with velocity in another group [S5]. In women, a later (more delayed) arm position at release was the strongest predictor [S9].
- **Physical qualities matter too.** Arm length (r≈0.65) and shoulder strength (r≈0.58) correlate with speed [S1]. Juniors' speed tracked jump power, upper-body power and muscle mass, and seniors' tracked jump power and arm length [S7].
- **Careful:** lateral trunk flexion (side-bend) is also linked to back injury [S19][S20][S30]. Don't coach "lean over more" for pace. Coach "chest drives forward over a firm front leg, arm comes late".
- **Separation ≠ counter-rotation.** *Late* separation (hips open before the shoulders, at front-foot contact) went with speed [S4]. *Counter-rotation* (shoulders twisting away from the target after back-foot contact) went with stress fractures [S4]. Greater hip–shoulder separation already at *back-foot* contact was also seen in bowlers with soft-tissue injuries [S4]. So the cue is "late", not "more twist".

**Analogy.** Cracking a whip. The handle (legs) stops sharply and the energy races down to the tip (hand and ball). If the handle keeps moving, there's no crack.

**Sport moments (mascot-actable).** The `lineup` L4 mascot, then a slow-mo `mascot` sequence: plant (flash) → hips open → chest drives down → arm whips over → long follow-through. A speedometer climbs at each link.

**Training methods + dosage.**
1. **Plyometrics (best-supported speed method).** In fast bowlers, plyometric training had a **moderate, significant effect on bowling speed (SMD 0.75)**. Resistance training alone (SMD 0.40) and modified-ball-weight training (SMD 0.30) were small and non-significant [S11]. Start at **60–100 foot contacts per session, 2×/week, 48+ h apart**. Lower volumes worked as well as double the volume [S28]. Examples: pogo hops → bounding → lateral bounds → skater jumps onto a braced landing **(exercise choice coach-derived)**.
2. **Med-ball power circuit (the club-bowler protocol).** In club bowlers (~87–91 km/h at baseline), a **4-week, 2×/week** programme added **~5 km/h (~6%)** with no loss of accuracy. The control group gained 0 [S8]. Exact dose [S8]:
   - Bodyweight squats **2 × 10→12**, hip raises **2 × 10→12**, split squats **2 × 6→8**
   - Med-ball chest pass, recoil overhead slam, rotational side toss/shot-put, step-behind throws: **2 × 6→8**
   - **1 min** between sets, done alongside normal nets
   - Plus over/underweight balls (±10–15%), 24–30 balls per session. Small trial (9 per group). Present it as "one small study", not a guarantee.
3. **General strength, 2×/week:** squat/split-squat pattern, hip hinge, pull-ups/rows, pressing, and trunk work. Use **2–4 sets × 8–12** at 60–70% 1RM, with 2–3 min rest and 48 h between sessions for the same muscles. Novices can start with a single set [S27]. Strength training also cut sports injuries to under a third in RCTs (RR 0.315; not cricket-specific) [S29].
4. **Weighted balls: optional, cautious.** Speed drops ~1.1 m/s per +100 g, and accuracy and mechanics weren't harmed in one small study [S12]. But the pooled effect on speed is small and non-significant [S11]. Keep within ±10–15% of match weight as in [S8], count those balls in your workload, and only do it under a coach **(caution coach-derived)**.

**Not suitable / caution:** plyometrics and heavy lifting are for healthy players with a base of strength. Under-18s should be supervised by a qualified coach (general caution). Anyone with back pain, a previous stress fracture or a recent growth spurt should get physio/coach sign-off first [S23]. Expect training effects over weeks: [S8] used 4 weeks, and [S28] found jump gains appeared 6–14 days after a 6-week block.

**Common mistakes.**
- "Just bowl more to bowl faster." Volume spikes are the main injury trigger [S13][S15], and the speed evidence favours power training [S8][S11].
- Leaning sideways for extra pace. Side-bend is linked to back injury [S19][S20].
- Expecting the gym to transfer automatically. Resistance training alone showed a small, non-significant effect on speed in the meta-analysis [S11]. Combine it with power work and bowling [S8].

## Level 5: The Durable Strike Bowler (workload management)

**Explanation.** Fast bowlers are the most injured players in cricket. In Australian elite cricket, ~20.6% of fast bowlers were unavailable at any time, against 12.5% of all players [S16]. **Lumbar stress fractures** are the most prevalent injury, accounting for ~15% of all missed playing time [S16]. They cluster in **young** bowlers: 18–22-year-olds had the highest rate (4.90 per 100 bowlers per year), and 74% happened in under-25s [S15]. Adolescents are at higher risk for **2–3 years after their main growth spurt** [S23].

**What spikes do (the core beat).**
- Elite bowlers whose **1-week load was more than double (>200%) their 4-week average** had **3.3× (balls bowled) to 4.5× (effort-based load)** the injury risk the following week [S13].
- In English county bowlers, a **peak 7-day load of 234+ deliveries (~39 overs)** carried **~11× the odds** of a lumbar stress fracture compared with <197 deliveries [S15].
- Bowling more than 50 overs in a match was followed by raised injury risk over the next 2–4 weeks, which shows the delay between load and injury [S13, citing Orchard 2009].
- **Too little hurts too:** higher chronic (4-week) bowling load went with *lower* injury risk [S13]. An elite study found a "window" of ~123–188 deliveries a week, with higher risk both above and below it [S13, citing Dennis 2003]. Low loads leave bowlers underprepared [S16].
- **Rest days matter:** juniors averaging fewer than 3.5 days between bowling days had **3.1×** the injury risk [S14]. In elite adults, both <2 days and >5 days between bowling sessions raised risk [S13, citing Dennis 2003].

**ACWR caveat (must be in the script if "ratio" is mentioned).** The acute:chronic workload ratio is widely used, and a 2026 meta-analysis pooled RR 2.45 for "elevated" ratios with high heterogeneity [S26]. But leading methodologists argue that the ratio has statistical artefacts and **no established causal link**, and they say it shouldn't drive injury-reduction recommendations [S25]. Safe framing: **"avoid sudden jumps in how much you bowl; build up gradually"**. Don't say "keep your ratio under 1.5".

**Analogy.** Bone is like a savings account. Regular deposits (gradual bowling) build the balance. A huge withdrawal in one week (a spike) bounces, and the bill arrives two or three weeks later.

**Sport moments (mascot-actable).** A calendar with bowling days and rest days. A battery icon. The mascot bowls on day 1, then on day 2 does strength or batting/fielding (non-bowling days can include these [S23]). A spike scene: a bar chart with one tall red week, then the mascot holding its lower back (`hands_back`/ouch pose), with a "3–4× risk" stat.

**Official junior limits (for 16–19-year-old viewers; quote as "under-19 rules", not adult advice).**

| Body | Age | Max overs / spell | Max overs / day | Weekly / rest rules |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ECB (2025, mandatory, recreational hardball) [S24] | 14–15 | 5 | 12 | Rest after a spell until the same number of overs is bowled from that end |
|  | 16–17 | 6 | 15 | Near-match-intensity training balls count |
|  | 18–19 | 7 | 18 | |
| Cricket Australia (2026) [S23] | U17 ("1-3-5") | 5 | 16 (15–17 match) | Rest day after each bowling day; max 3 bowling days/week; 20 min between training spells |
|  | 17–19 ("2-4-6") | 6 | 18 | Max 2 bowling days in a row; max 4 bowling days/week |

Cricket Australia also recommends a **6–8-week pre-season build-up** for 15–19-year-olds (a full build-up is usually >10 weeks), a **light week every 4–6 weeks**, **7–10 days off bowling every 10–12 weeks**, and ~3 months off at season's end [S23].

**Training methods + dosage.**
1. **Count your balls.** Log every near-full-effort delivery (match + nets) per week [S13][S24]. **Rule (coach-derived from [S13][S15][S23]):** increase weekly balls gradually and avoid a week that is roughly double your recent average. Plan a lighter week every 4–6 weeks [S23].
2. **Spread your bowling days.** Juniors follow the CA 1-3-5 / 2-4-6 rules [S23]. For adults, aim for **2–3 bowling days a week with a day off between** **(coach-derived; the risk patterns for <2 and >5 days between sessions are from elite data [S13])**.
3. **Pre-season build-up.** Don't go from zero to match spells. Over **6–10 weeks** [S23], grow from short spells at reduced effort to full match spells **(the exact steps are coach-derived)**. Example: weeks 1–2: 2 sessions × 3–4 overs at 70–80%; weeks 3–4: 2–3 sessions × 4–5 overs, some at full pace; weeks 5–6: 3 sessions × 5–6 overs plus a practice match; then match spells.
4. **Back-and-hip "armour".** Injured adolescent bowlers had **lower back-extensor endurance (103 vs 132 s)** and more knee valgus on single-leg squats [S20]. An 8-week exercise programme for 14–17-year-old community bowlers improved back endurance by **~20 s**, hip and hamstring strength, and balance [S21]. It also **reduced counter-rotation (~4°) and side-bend (~2°) without slowing the ball** [S22]. Suggested circuit **(coach-derived)**: back-extension hold or "superman" holds 3 × 20–40 s, side plank 3 × 20–30 s per side, single-leg squat to a box 2–3 × 8 per leg (knee over toes, no caving in), and glute bridge 3 × 10–12. Do it **2–3×/week**. Note that this programme **did not measure injuries** [S21][S22].

**Common mistakes.**
- **"Bowling through it."** Back bone stress injuries often develop **before** pain is felt, so new back pain while bowling is a stop sign [S23].
- **The early-season or comeback spike.** Lumbar stress fractures peaked when fixtures were condensed in a bunched schedule [S15]. Spikes are riskier than high-but-steady loads [S13][S16].
- **Resting completely, then bowling a long spell.** Low chronic load is also risky [S13][S16].

## Put it together: sample week for a club bowler (coach-derived)

| Day | Session |
|---|---|
| Mon | Strength + plyometrics (Level 3–4: split squats, med-ball circuit, 60–80 contacts) |
| Tue | Nets: run-up rhythm + 4–6 overs (count balls) |
| Wed | Rest or easy run + back/hip circuit (Level 5) |
| Thu | Strength (lighter) + back/hip circuit |
| Fri | Rest from bowling (batting/fielding fine [S23]) |
| Sat | Match |
| Sun | Rest |

Rules: strength 2×/week, 48 h apart [S27]; plyos ≤2×/week, 48 h+ apart [S28]; a day off bowling after each bowling day where possible [S14][S23]; no big weekly jumps in balls bowled [S13][S15]; light week every 4–6 weeks [S23]. Juniors: follow ECB/CA limits [S23][S24].

## Myths

1. **"Speed comes from the arm."** Physical and whole-body variables (run-up, front-leg brace, sequence) correlate with speed more than arm-only ones [S1][S2][S3].
2. **"A mixed action / big twist gives you extra pace."** Counter-rotation isn't associated with ball speed [S5], but it is linked to stress fractures and back pain [S4][S18]. All risk, no reward.
3. **"Side-on is the only correct action."** Side-on and front-on are both recognised. The risk comes from *mixing* them (counter-rotation) [S17].
4. **"Bowl as much as you can to get better."** Big spikes raise injury risk 3–4× [S13], and 234+ deliveries in a week raised stress-fracture odds ~11× [S15]. The speed evidence favours power training [S8][S11].
5. **"Rest is always safer."** Very low chronic load and long gaps between sessions are also linked to injury. Build up and stay consistent [S13][S16].
6. **"No pain, no problem."** Back bone stress injuries often develop before pain is felt [S23]. MRI bone stress precedes fractures [S18][S30].
7. **"Lock your front knee hard."** A firm front knee is linked to speed [S1][S3], but it raises impact forces [S6][S19]. In women, faster bowlers sometimes had more knee bend [S10]. Earn it with strength and progress it.
8. **"Weighted balls are the secret."** The pooled effect is small and non-significant [S11]. One small study showed gains in some players [S12].
9. **"Stretching prevents injuries."** Not in RCT pooling (RR 0.96). Strength training does (RR 0.32) [S29][S27].
10. **"The ACWR sweet spot (0.8–1.3) is proven."** Contested. There's no established causal link [S25]. Use the plain rule "avoid sudden jumps".

## Safety (for the script and the fact-checker)

- **General:** educational content for healthy amateurs, not medical advice [S27]. Anyone with current back, knee, shoulder or ankle pain should see a physio or sports doctor before increasing bowling or adding plyometrics.
- **Red flag 1 (must be in the video):** **lower back pain while or after bowling, especially on one side.** Stop bowling and see a sports doctor or physio before bowling again. Bone stress injuries can develop before pain [S23]. Fractures are mainly at L4–L5 [S15], and pars abnormalities are mostly on the non-bowling-arm side [S17]. (Keep it general: "one-sided low back pain that comes with bowling: get it checked". No diagnosis.)
- **High-risk groups needing extra caution:** teenagers and under-25s [S15][S19]; the 2–3 years after a growth spurt; tall or fast-for-age bowlers; and anyone with a previous back bone stress injury. These need *more* recovery than standard limits and should involve a medical professional [S23].
- **Action changes:** remodelling a mixed action should be done with a qualified coach and done gradually. It hasn't been shown prospectively to reduce fractures [S16], and evidence on the action label is mixed [S19].
- **Front-leg brace:** progress at reduced intensity with strength work. Don't force a locked knee at full pace (impact 5–9× body mass) [S6][S19].
- **Workload:** juniors must follow the ECB/CA limits (ECB limits are mandatory for U19 recreational hardball in England & Wales) [S23][S24]. Everyone should avoid sudden spikes, build up for 6–10 weeks pre-season, and take a light week every 4–6 weeks [S13][S15][S23].
- **Strength and plyos:** ACSM ranges [S27]; novice plyo volume ~60–100 contacts, 2×/week [S28]. Under-18s should be supervised by a qualified coach (general caution). Warm up first (dynamic warm-up as in [S28]'s protocol).
- **Weighted balls:** only small changes (±10–15%) and under a coach. Count them as bowling load [S8][S11][S12].
- **No supplements or medical treatments** are discussed or recommended.
